Act 1 · Learn

What does it mean to learn from data?

Classical analysis writes the rules. ML learns them.

  • Left to right in the top lane: the human writes the rule, the computer applies it. Bottom lane (click): the human supplies examples and answers; the optimiser finds the rule.
  • The one point to land: ML does not remove domain knowledge. The physicist still decides what x and y mean — and, as we see today, which loss to minimise.
  • Misconception: “ML finds everything by itself.” It learns only patterns that are in the training data. Biased or incomplete data give biased, incomplete rules.
  • “All models are wrong, but some are useful” (Box). A model is a purposeful abstraction Neuer, Michael et al., (2024); usefulness is judged at the decision point.
  • When first principles are not enough: multislice simulation of a thick specimen exists, but it is too slow for 65 536 patterns of a 4D-STEM scan. Hybrid strategy: keep trusted physics as structure, learn the residual.

Three ways to learn — set by where the labels come from

  • Same specimen throughout: a 4D-STEM scan of a multi-phase alloy.
  • Supervised: an expert labelled 1000 diffraction patterns; the model generalises. Label cost is the practical driver — hours per labelled EM image.
  • Unsupervised: no labels; k-means finds groups that turn out to be phases. K is a materials decision, not a mathematical one (Week 3 builds PCA + k-means).
  • Self-supervised (Noise2Noise Lehtinen, Jaakko et al., (2018)): two noisy frames of the same area; one is the input, the other the target. Because the noise is zero-mean given the clean image, the MSE minimiser is the clean image. Odd/even frames of a cryo-EM movie are a ready-made pair. It fails for coherent artefacts (drift, Moiré).
  • Demo: in “Self-supervised”, drag N up — the mean of noisy targets converges to the clean lattice as 1/√N.
  • Forward links: Week 7 (CNNs, mostly supervised), Week 8 (self-supervision), Week 9 (autoencoders).

More flexibility is not better: bias vs variance

  • Data: a synthetic carbon K-edge (EELS) with Poisson noise. Fits: polynomials of degree 0–15 and the physical edge model (power-law background + edge step).
  • Demo: start at degree 15 (wiggles through the noise), go to 0 (misses the edge), find the minimum of the error on new data.
  • The one point to land: training error always falls with complexity; error on new data has a minimum. No model has zero bias and zero variance.
  • The physical model (dashed green) beats every polynomial on new data: physics priors cut variance without adding bias. This is why physics matters so much for small EM data sets.
  • Formal decomposition (bias² + variance + noise) and regularisation: Week 4.

White, grey or black box: how much physics is built in?

  • White box: explicit mechanism, interpretable parameters (Bragg’s law, Beer–Lambert). Black box: flexible, no traceable mechanism (deep networks). Grey box: physics structure + learned parts (PINNs, learned hardening law in crystal plasticity) Neuer, Michael et al., (2024).
  • The box colour is not a quality label. AlphaFold is black-box and won a Nobel Prize; Bragg’s law is white-box and cannot predict if an amorphous film crystallises under the beam.
  • Why black-box criticism is engineering, not philosophy: a CNN classifies phases with 97 % accuracy, but cannot say if it learned lattice geometry or the beam-stop shadow. When the artefact changes (other microscope), it fails silently.
  • Good practice: check that the model learned the right features, not only the right score. Tools come with each model family (Weeks 4, 5, 7) and are synthesised in Week 13.

Act 2 · Signal

What does an EM pixel measure?

Every EM number passes through six links

The model sees only the end of the chain. What the chain destroys, no model can recover without a forward model or a prior.

  • Click through the six links, left to right. One EM example per link (they appear in the panel).
  • The one point to land: the measured array y is a random draw from a physics-determined distribution, not the specimen. Once we write y ~ p(y | …), the loss follows as −log p — that is Act 5.
  • Misconception: “the file link is bureaucracy.” Without pixel size and dose, the same image can be a 2 Å lattice or a 6 Å alias; without the gain, numbers cannot become counts, and the Poisson model is unusable.
  • Forward link: Weeks 12/13 write this chain as a forward operator A and invert it (deblurring, tomography, ptychography).

Four modalities, four tensor shapes

  • HAADF: the probe dwells 10–100 µs per position; the annular detector counts high-angle scattered electrons; intensity ∝ Z^1.6–1.9 (Z² in the Rutherford limit). Each pixel = dwell time × current × cross-section = a count of electrons. That sentence leads to Poisson statistics in Act 4 Ophus, Colin, (2023).
  • 4D-STEM: a diffraction pattern at every probe position; 256 × 256 × 128 × 128 × 4 bytes ≈ 4.3 GB per scan. Demo the size sliders.
  • EELS / EDS spectrum image: a spectrum at every pixel; PCA and NMF in Week 3.
  • Tomography: the 3-D volume comes from the inverse step (Weeks 12/13); the raw data are 2-D projections.
  • The one point to land: the modality sets the tensor shape, the shape sets the architecture family. Flattening a 4D-STEM cube loses the real-space / reciprocal-space coupling.

Act 3 · Sampling

Is the pixel grid fine enough?

Nyquist: at least two pixels per period — better four

  • Nyquist–Shannon: a signal with frequencies up to f_max is fully recorded if f_s ≥ 2 f_max. For an image, f_s = 1/Δx, so a period d needs Δx ≤ d/2.
  • Demo: start at 1.5 Å (alias), go to 1.0 Å (just at the limit), then to 0.5 Å (four pixels per period).
  • The one point to land: Nyquist is a lower bound, not a target. Probe size, PSF and noise all eat resolution, so sample 3–4× finer (pixel ≤ a/4 for atomic resolution).
  • Nyquist applies everywhere in EM: real-space pixels, EELS energy channels, reciprocal-space pixels (camera length), tilt steps in tomography.

Undersampling turns a 2 Å lattice into a false 6 Å lattice

  • Work the numbers once: a = 2 Å → k = 5 nm⁻¹. Δx = 1.5 Å → f_s = 6.67 nm⁻¹, recordable range ±3.33 nm⁻¹. The spot folds to |5 − 6.67| = 1.67 nm⁻¹ → a false 6 Å lattice.
  • Rule: alias frequency = |f − n f_s| for the nearest integer n.
  • Demo: rotate the specimen. The true spots rotate with it; the aliased spots move in a different, non-intuitive way. That is a diagnostic.
  • The one point to land: aliasing is coherent and deterministic. Averaging frames removes Poisson noise as 1/√N, but never removes an alias. Only a smaller pixel (or shorter camera length in diffraction) helps.
  • Forward link (Week 7): a CNN trained on aliased data learns to predict the alias.

Moiré fringes: real structure or artefact?

  • Two overlapping crystals with slightly different spacing or a small twist produce beats with a long period: D = a₁a₂/|a₁ − a₂| (mismatch) or D = a / (2 sin(θ/2)) (twist).
  • Demo: press “rotate specimen by 1°”. The fringes rotate by much more than 1° — Moiré magnifies rotation. Real structure rotates with the specimen.
  • The one point to land: a long-period fringe is a Moiré candidate until proven otherwise (rotation test, diffraction).
  • Moiré is not always bad: in twisted bilayer graphene, the Moiré period measures the twist angle.

Act 4 · Counting

Why is every pixel noisy?

Electrons arrive one at a time: counts are Poisson

  • Let the animation run for 20 s without comment. Then: each electron arrives independently, at random. The number per pixel per frame follows a Poisson distribution.
  • Press “1000 frames”: the histogram matches the Poisson pmf, and variance / mean → 1.
  • The one point to land: Var(N) = λ, so SNR = λ/√λ = √λ. Doubling the dose improves the SNR by only √2 — the first of our three numbers.
  • Shot noise is the quantum limit. No detector removes it. A perfect direct detector reaches it.

Twice the SNR costs four times the dose

  • The phantom: atomic columns on a lattice, plus one weak (light-element / dopant) column. Noise: np.random.poisson(dose × phantom).
  • Demo: go down to ~5 counts at a column peak; the columns vanish. Find the dose at which the weak column is lost (CNR < 3). This is the notebook exercise.
  • Typical doses: ~1000 e⁻/Å² for metals, ~10 e⁻/Å² for beam-sensitive 2-D materials, 20–80 e⁻/Å² for cryo-EM. At 10 e⁻/Å² and 0.1 Å pixels, λ ≈ 0.1 counts per pixel.
  • Misconception: “a strong denoiser recovers everything.” It can only fill in what its prior expects — at the risk of hallucination. Information that was never recorded is gone.

The detector blurs: scintillator vs direct electron detector

  • Indirect (scintillator + CMOS): electron → light → sensor. Light spreads → broad PSF, lower DQE, read noise. Robust and cheap.
  • Direct electron detector: the electron deposits charge in a thin Si layer → sharp PSF, single-electron counting, noise close to pure Poisson. Enabled the cryo-EM “resolution revolution” (Nobel Prize 2017). Sensor damage over time; expensive.
  • Demo: switch presets, look at the MTF at the lattice frequency and at the close column pair.
  • The one point to land: the detector decides the noise model. A denoiser trained on K3 counting data and used on a scintillator camera meets other statistics — a silent domain shift.

Real detectors add Gaussian read noise

\(x = g\cdot\text{Poisson}(\lambda) + \mathcal N(0, \sigma_r^2)\), with gain \(g\) (ADU per electron) and read noise \(\sigma_r\).

  • Gain g (ADU per electron) scales the counts; read noise σr is Gaussian, independent of the signal.
  • Variance–mean relation: Var(x) = g·x̄ + σr². It holds for every preset.
  • Demo: “direct detector, counting” — single-electron peaks are separate: you can count. “CCD, high read noise” — the peaks merge into one Gaussian.
  • The one point to land: σr ≪ g → counting regime, Poisson model. σr ≳ g → mixed model. Know which one your data are in before you choose the loss.

Measure your detector: the variance–mean plot

  • Recipe: flat-field frames at several illuminations → per-pixel mean and variance → linear fit. Slope = gain g, intercept = read-noise variance σr². Five lines of NumPy: stack, mean(axis=0), var(axis=0), np.polyfit.
  • Log-log view: read-noise floor at low signal, shot noise (slope 1) in the middle, fixed-pattern noise (slope 2) at high signal → flat-field correction needed.
  • Misconception: “digital numbers are electron counts.” Subtract the offset and divide by g first — otherwise the Poisson loss sees numbers that are not counts.
  • Typical values: counting direct detectors g ≈ 1 count/e⁻, negligible read noise. Integrating CMOS: σr of several electrons.
  • Notebook Part 7 recovers g and σr from simulated frames.

Act 5 · Loss

Which loss does the physics dictate?

Learning = maximum likelihood

\(\displaystyle \hat\theta = \arg\max_\theta \prod_i p\big(y_i \mid f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i)\big) = \arg\min_\theta \sum_i \underbrace{-\log p\big(y_i \mid f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i)\big)}_{\color{#f43f5e}{\text{loss } \ell}}\)

  • Empirical risk minimisation (MFML Unit 1) picks θ by minimising the average loss. Where does ℓ come from? Write the measurement as a random draw y ~ p(y | f_θ(x)) — the data-formation chain as a probability model.
  • Demo: move λ̂. Each red stem is the probability of one observation. The product of eight small numbers is tiny (10⁻⁸): take the log. The log is monotone, so the argmax does not move.
  • The minimum is at the sample mean, for both the Poisson and the Gaussian model — but the curves have different shapes. The shape is the weighting; that matters when a model must compromise across pixels.
  • Recipe for the next slides: noise model → likelihood → −log → drop terms without θ → loss.
  • Independence assumption: pixels are independent given the clean signal. Good for direct detectors; for scintillators the PSF correlates neighbours.

Gaussian noise → mean squared error

1 · noise model

\(y_i = \mu_i + \varepsilon_i,\;\; \varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2)\)

\(p(y_i\mid\mu_i) = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\, e^{-\frac{(y_i-\mu_i)^2}{2\sigma^2}}\)

2 · take −log

\(-\log p = \dfrac{(y_i-\mu_i)^2}{2\sigma^2} + \tfrac12 \log 2\pi\sigma^2\)

3 · drop terms without θ

\(\color{#94a3b8}{\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}}\,(y_i-\mu_i)^2\; \color{#94a3b8}{+\,\tfrac12\log 2\pi\sigma^2}\)

\(\Rightarrow\;\; \ell_\text{MSE} = (y_i - \mu_i)^2\)

Every nn.MSELoss() claims: Gaussian, independent, same σ for every pixel. True left of the crossover on the variance–mean plot; false for shot noise.

  • Three lines on the board; two minutes. Stress the step “if σ² is the same for every pixel” — that is where the physics enters.
  • Known σᵢ per pixel → weighted least squares, weights 1/σᵢ². Model predicts σᵢ² → heteroscedastic Gaussian NLL (nn.GaussianNLLLoss), the basis of aleatoric uncertainty in Week 11.
  • Heavy tails (X-ray hits, hot pixels) → Huber loss or Student-t NLL; MAE is the NLL of a Laplace distribution.
  • History: Gauss asked which error distribution makes the arithmetic mean the best estimate. The answer is the normal distribution. MSE and the Gaussian belong together by definition.

Poisson noise → Poisson negative log-likelihood

1 · noise model

\(y_i \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_i),\;\; \lambda_i = f_\theta(\mathbf x_i) > 0\)

\(p(y_i\mid\lambda_i) = \dfrac{\lambda_i^{\,y_i}\, e^{-\lambda_i}}{y_i!}\)

2 · take −log

\(-\log p = \lambda_i - y_i \log \lambda_i + \log(y_i!)\)

3 · drop terms without θ

\(\lambda_i - y_i\log\lambda_i\; \color{#94a3b8}{+\,\log(y_i!)}\)

\(\Rightarrow\;\; \ell_\text{Pois} = \lambda_i - y_i \log\lambda_i\)

Gradient: \(\;\partial\ell_\text{Pois}/\partial\lambda = (\lambda - y)/\lambda\;\) = MSE gradient ÷ variance. The Poisson loss weights each pixel by 1/variance — automatically.

  • y must be a count in electrons: subtract the offset, divide by the gain first.
  • Compare: ∂ℓ_MSE/∂λ = 2(λ − y); ∂ℓ_Pois/∂λ = 1 − y/λ = (λ − y)/λ. Built-in inverse-variance weighting that adapts as the model improves.
  • PyTorch: predict z = log λ and use nn.PoissonNLLLoss(log_input=True) — numerically the most stable. Or λ = softplus(z) and loss = λ − y·log(λ + ε).
  • Misconception: “at high counts Poisson becomes Gaussian, so MSE is fine.” It becomes Gaussian with variance λ — still signal-dependent. MSE is fine only if λ is nearly constant over the image.

Same minimum, different weights

  • Demo: x = 8. Both minima are at λ̂ = 8, but the Poisson curve is 16× flatter (curvature 1/8 vs 2): a bright pixel pulls on the fit much less than in MSE.
  • x = 0 (dark pixel): MSE punishes only λ̂², tiny near zero; Poisson punishes λ̂ linearly and forbids λ̂ = 0 where an electron arrived.
  • The one point to land: per pixel the minimiser is the same, but a model shares parameters across pixels; the loss decides how the compromise is weighted. MSE spends its effort on the bright atomic columns; the dim background and light-element columns — vacancies, dopants — get almost no weight.

Variance-stabilising transform: Poisson → ≈ Gaussian

  • Anscombe Anscombe, Francis J., (1948), doi:10.1093/biomet/35.3-4.246: z = 2√(y + 3/8) → z is approximately Gaussian with σ ≈ 1 for λ ≳ 4. Generalised Anscombe adds read noise (needs g and σr from the variance–mean plot).
  • Then use MSE on z, or any Gaussian tool (PCA, BM3D, standard CNN denoisers), and invert with an unbiased inverse.
  • The one point to land: a VST fixes the variance, not the bias. E[√y] ≠ √E[y]; at low counts the naive inverse is biased by 10–30 %. Below ~1–4 counts per pixel use the exact Poisson or Poisson–Gaussian NLL.
  • EM anchor: Anscombe + PCA is standard pre-processing for EELS/EDS spectrum images (Week 3).

Experiment: same spectrum, four losses, four answers

  • Setup: 64-channel spectrum, one Gaussian peak on a flat background; three parameters fitted with each loss; repeated for many synthetic spectra per dose.
  • Weighted χ² (Neyman, σᵢ² = yᵢ, the textbook recipe) and naive Anscombe are biased low by 10–20 % at a few counts per pixel — a systematic error in every quantified EELS/EDS map. This is the third of our three numbers.
  • Plain MSE is unbiased here (linear in amplitude and background) but inefficient: about 1.5× larger scatter → the same precision costs ~50 % more dose.
  • Poisson NLL: unbiased and most precise. The ML estimate conserves the total counts (Σλ̂ = Σy), so integrated intensities come out right.
  • The one point to land: with the same data, only the loss changes, and the answer moves by 10–20 %. At low dose the loss is a larger error source than most knobs people tune.
  • Notebook Parts 8–9 reproduce this experiment.

Which loss? Follow the physics

  • Ask the room for each leaf: HAADF on an integrating camera at high dose (MSE); EELS SI with measured per-channel variance (weighted MSE); strain with uncertainty (Gaussian NLL, Week 11); counting-mode 4D-STEM (Poisson NLL); low-dose CMOS frames (VST + MSE); X-ray hits (Huber); phase classification (cross-entropy, Week 6); nanoparticle masks (BCE / Dice, Week 7).
  • The one point to land: cross-entropy is the NLL of a categorical distribution, MAE of a Laplace distribution. One principle, not a zoo of losses.
  • The full table is on the backup slide “Cheat sheet”.

Priors become regularisers: MAP estimation

\(\displaystyle \hat\theta_\text{MAP} = \arg\min_\theta \Big[\underbrace{-\log p(\mathcal D\mid\theta)}_{\text{data term: today's loss}}\;\underbrace{-\log p(\theta)}_{\color{#f43f5e}{\text{prior → regulariser}}}\Big]\)

  • Bayes (MFML Unit 1): p(θ | D) ∝ p(D | θ) p(θ). Maximise the posterior = minimise data term + regulariser.
  • Gaussian prior → L2 / weight decay; Laplace prior → L1 / sparsity; smoothness prior → total variation; non-negativity → NMF (Week 3).
  • Demo: atoms are sparse spikes. L2 spreads intensity over neighbours; L1 finds the columns. Move λ: too small → noise, too large → columns vanish.
  • The one point to land: physics enters twice — the detector decides the data term, specimen knowledge decides the prior. Full treatment: Week 4 (ridge / lasso), Week 12 (TV, plug-and-play priors).

Act 6 · Trust

Can I trust the data and the model?

Eight artefacts — and the plot that catches each

  • Adapted from ML-PC Unit 3 (“six failure modes”), in EM language. Each artefact maps to a link of the chain.
  • Toggle the artefact off: learn what a normal histogram, FFT and frame series look like. Then on.
  • Quick checks that catch most problems in minutes: pixel histogram (saturation, dead pixels), counts per frame (beam damage, blanking), FFT (aliasing, scan noise), cross-correlation of frames (drift, duplicates), variance–mean plot (wrong gain reference).
  • The one point to land: clean at the source first. Digital repair cannot recover what was never recorded, and every repair changes the noise model (interpolated pixels are not Poisson). Mask, do not silently interpolate.
  • Misconception: “remove outliers.” One bright pixel may be an X-ray hit (remove) or a heavy dopant atom (the discovery). Decide with physics, not with a z-score.
  • Duplicates and overlapping crops are the #1 source of leakage in EM ML papers (Week 4, GroupKFold).

Metadata: the measurement you forget to save

  • Ask: who has a folder of TIFF screenshots without pixel size? For quantitative ML, that folder is lost.
  • Demo: delete the gain → no counts → no Poisson loss. Delete the pixel size → no Nyquist check. Delete the specimen ID → no honest split. Then “export as TIFF”.
  • The one point to land: every field is needed by some step of today’s lecture. Metadata is part of the measurement.
  • Keep the native file (.dm4, .emd, .mrc) and read it with open readers (HyperSpy, RosettaSciIO, py4DSTEM, LiberTEM).

Payoff for ML: reusable training data, reproducible splits, models you can re-validate on someone else’s microscope.

  • FAIR is about machine-actionability — exactly what an ML pipeline needs. Most funders (DFG, EU) now require it.
  • FAIR does not mean public: “Accessible” allows restricted data with open metadata.
  • EM success story: EMPIAR (raw cryo-EM movies) enabled new motion-correction and denoising methods by groups that never used the microscope.
  • One concrete action per letter: DOI via Zenodo; open metadata; EMD/HDF5 instead of TIFF; a processing script instead of manual clicks.

Aleatoric vs epistemic: what more data can fix

  • Aleatoric (irreducible): randomness of the process — shot noise, thermal vibration. More frames do not shrink the spread of one frame.
  • Epistemic (reducible): lack of knowledge — unknown gain or PSF, too little training data, a wrong model. More data or better calibration does reduce it Neuer, Michael et al., (2024).
  • Demo: move N. The count spread stays at √λ = 5; the gain error falls as 1/√N.
  • Decision rule: “Can I reduce this uncertainty?” Yes → epistemic, act on it. No → aleatoric, propagate it honestly (error bars, noise-matched losses, no over-denoising).
  • Trust teaser: a model can have a low training loss and be physically wrong. Trust needs the right noise model, no leakage, held-out tests, interpretable features and physics checks. Active learning (Week 11) targets epistemic uncertainty.

Pre-flight checklist: run it before any training

  • Aviation and surgery use checklists because experts skip steps under time pressure. Microscope time is always under pressure.
  • Every item takes minutes; each prevents a failure that costs weeks. Item 2 is today’s core: without it, the loss is a guess.
  • Students run this checklist on their miniproject data in Week 3 and report it in the proposal. The rubric awards points for a documented data-quality check and a justified loss.

Must-know for the exam

1 A loss is the negative log-likelihood of a noise model. MSE ⇔ Gaussian with constant variance.

2 Poisson counts: Var = mean, SNR = √λ. Twice the SNR needs 4× the dose.

3 Poisson loss ℓ = λ − y log λ weights each pixel by 1/variance.

4 At low counts, χ² with observed-count weights and naive Anscombe are biased; Poisson NLL is not.

5 Variance–mean plot: slope = gain, intercept = read-noise variance.

6 Sample at ≥ 2× (in practice 3–4×) the highest frequency. Aliasing does not average out.

7 Without dose, gain and pixel size in the metadata, EM data cannot be modelled quantitatively (FAIR).

Supervised learning: regression vs classification

  • Regression: continuous target \(y \in \mathbb{R}\) — strain from a 4D-STEM pattern, atomic-column positions. Loss: MSE or Poisson NLL.
  • Classification: discrete label \(y \in \{1,\ldots,K\}\) — oxide / metal / carbide from EELS. Loss: cross-entropy.
  • The physical quantity sets the output type: a “phase index of 2.7” is nonsense; binning strain throws resolution away.

Self-supervised learning: why Noise2Noise works

  • Train a denoiser on pairs of noisy images of the same scene Lehtinen, Jaakko et al., (2018).
  • If the noise is zero-mean given the clean signal, \(\mathbb{E}[y_2 \mid x] = s\), so the MSE minimiser for target \(y_2\) is the clean image \(s\).
  • Works for Poisson, Gaussian, salt-and-pepper noise. Fails for coherent artefacts (drift, Moiré): they are the same in both frames.
  • EM: odd/even frames of a counting-mode movie are a ready-made pair (Week 8).
