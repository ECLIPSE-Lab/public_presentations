## Week 1 — Python crash course & the EM data deluge

1. **Data-rate growth:** modern 4D pixelated detectors produce up to ~200 TB h⁻¹ — approximately 10⁸× more than film-era instruments — making automated analysis unavoidable.
2. **PSPP chain:** Processing → Structure → Property → Performance; each arrow is a distinct ML task; structure *mediates* properties and cannot be skipped in the causal chain.
3. **CRISP-DM phases (in order):** Business/scientific understanding → Data understanding → Data preparation → Modelling → Evaluation → Deployment/monitoring; evaluation measures *generalisation*, not training accuracy.
4. **NumPy array fundamentals:** every EM dataset is an ndarray with a `shape` (e.g. `(512, 512)` for HAADF, `(256, 256, 128, 128)` for 4D-STEM) and a `dtype` (e.g. `float32`); these two attributes are always the first things to check.
5. **Vectorised operations vs. loops:** NumPy arithmetic applies element-wise to whole arrays without Python for-loops, giving ≥100× speed improvement; write `corrected = image - dark` not a pixel-by-pixel loop.
6. **Broadcasting rule:** two dimensions are compatible if they are equal or one of them is 1; shapes are aligned from the *right*; e.g. `(512, 512) − (512, 1)` is valid and subtracts each row mean from every column.
7. **Indexing is 0-based and slice stop is exclusive:** `arr[1:3]` returns elements at indices 1 and 2; `arr[-1]` is the last element; applies to all axes of an ndarray.
8. **Tensor = n-dimensional array:** a scalar is 0-D, a spectrum is 1-D, an image is 2-D, an EELS map is 3-D (x, y, energy), a 4D-STEM scan is 4-D (x, y, kx, ky); NumPy ndarray and PyTorch Tensor are both tensors.
9. **Min-max normalisation:** `(x − x.min()) / (x.max() − x.min())` scales any array to [0, 1]; standard pre-processing step before displaying or comparing EM images; implemented as a one-liner or a documented `def normalize(image)` function.
10. **Assessment structure:** 40% miniproject (reproducible notebook + report, CRISP-DM pipeline, uncertainty + explainability required) + 60% written exam; miniproject proposal due ≈ Week 6; a notebook that does not execute end-to-end scores zero on the reproducibility criterion (15% of miniproject grade).

## Week 2 — Learning from EM data: signal formation, noise & loss

1. Machine learning infers the input–output function from data; classical analysis applies human-written rules. Supervised / unsupervised / self-supervised differ by where the supervision signal comes from; white / grey / black-box by how traceable the mechanism is.
2. Every EM number passes through the data-formation chain specimen → interaction → detector → digitisation → metadata; noise and systematic errors enter at each link, and the model only sees the end of the chain.
3. Nyquist–Shannon: the pixel size must be at most half the smallest period of interest (in practice 3–4× oversampling); undersampling aliases lattice frequencies into spurious Moiré fringes that averaging cannot remove.
4. Electron counts are Poisson distributed: Var = mean = λ, SNR = √λ; doubling the SNR requires four times the dose.
5. A real detector adds Gaussian read noise: x = g·Poisson(λ) + N(0, σ_r²), so Var(x) = g·mean + σ_r²; the variance–mean plot gives the gain (slope) and read-noise variance (intercept).
6. A loss function is the negative log-likelihood of an assumed noise model: Gaussian with constant variance → MSE; Poisson → ℓ = λ − y log λ; categorical → cross-entropy.
7. The Poisson loss weights each pixel by 1/variance (its gradient is the MSE gradient divided by λ); MSE over-weights bright pixels and under-weights weak, low-count features.
8. At low counts, χ² weighted by the observed counts and naive Anscombe + MSE are biased low; the Poisson maximum-likelihood fit is unbiased, most precise and conserves total counts. Variance-stabilising transforms (Anscombe, generalised Anscombe) work only above a few counts per pixel.
9. Priors become regularisers in MAP estimation: Gaussian prior → L2, Laplace prior → L1, smoothness → total variation.
10. Without dose, gain, pixel size and specimen/session IDs in the metadata, EM data cannot be modelled quantitatively or split honestly; FAIR = Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable.

## Week 3 — Linear algebra, PCA & spectral unmixing

1. A spectrum image $(n_y, n_x, D)$ is unfolded into a data matrix $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}$ (rows = pixels, columns = energy channels); every decomposition this week factorises $\mathbf{X}\approx$ (maps) × (spectra).
2. PCA = SVD of the **centered** data matrix, $\mathbf{X}-\bar{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^T$: rows of $\mathbf{V}^T$ are eigenspectra (loadings), $\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ are scores, $\sigma_k^2/(N-1)$ is the variance along PC $k$; the rank-$K$ truncation is the best rank-$K$ least-squares approximation (Eckart–Young).
3. PCA denoises because the signal is **low-rank** and noise is **full-rank**; choose $K$ where the scree plot leaves the flat noise floor — too few components bias the result, too many add noise back (U-shaped error).
4. PCA is least squares and implicitly assumes equal-variance Gaussian noise; Poisson counts must be **weighted** (e.g. divide by $\sqrt{\text{pixel total}\times\text{mean spectrum}}$) or variance-stabilised before PCA, otherwise bright channels dominate and weak edges are truncated.
5. EELS preprocessing order: energy alignment → spike removal → power-law background $AE^{-r}$ (pre-edge fit) → normalisation → Poisson weighting → decomposition; a derivative-shaped loading ($\propto dI/dE$) is the fingerprint of energy misalignment.
6. Linear mixing model: $\mathbf{x}_i=\sum_k a_{ik}\mathbf{m}_k$ with $a_{ik}\ge0$, $\sum_k a_{ik}=1$ — pixels lie in a simplex whose vertices are the pure endmember spectra; with known endmembers this is (non-negative / fully constrained) least squares.
7. NMF solves $\min\|\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{WH}\|$ with $\mathbf{W},\mathbf{H}\ge0$ (parts-based, no subtraction); multiplicative updates $\mathbf{H}\leftarrow\mathbf{H}\odot\mathbf{W}^T\mathbf{X}/(\mathbf{W}^T\mathbf{WH})$ keep factors non-negative and never increase the loss; NMF with KL divergence = Poisson maximum likelihood.
8. MCR-ALS alternates constrained least-squares updates of concentrations $\mathbf{C}$ and spectra $\mathbf{S}$ in $\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{CS}^T+\mathbf{E}$, adding physical constraints (non-negativity, closure, unimodality, known spectra, selectivity); NMF is MCR with non-negativity only.
9. NMF/MCR solutions are **not unique** (rotational ambiguity): $\mathbf{CS}^T=(\mathbf{CT})(\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{S}^T)$ fits equally well; symptoms are seed-dependent endmembers, remedies are physical constraints — always report them. PCA is unique (up to sign) only because orthogonality is imposed.
10. Truncation keeps variance, not importance: rare phases and dilute dopants can be erased and PCA filtering can bias quantities while looking precise — always inspect (Poisson-normalised) **residual maps**, which must look like spatially uncorrelated noise.

## Week 4 — Regression, optimisation & honest validation

1. Regression is empirical risk minimisation, $\hat{\mathbf{w}}=\arg\min_\mathbf{w}\hat R(\mathbf{w})$; minimising MSE is maximum likelihood under Gaussian noise (MAE ↔ Laplace noise, Huber = robust compromise for occasional outliers).
2. Gradient descent updates $\mathbf{w}_{t+1}=\mathbf{w}_t-\eta\nabla\hat R(\mathbf{w}_t)$; too large $\eta$ oscillates or diverges, too small crawls, and an ill-conditioned loss ($\kappa(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X})\gg1$, e.g. unscaled or collinear features) makes GD zigzag — standardise inputs.
3. A convex loss has every local minimum global (chord above the curve, Hessian $\succeq 0$); linear models with MSE/MAE/Huber plus Ridge/Lasso are convex, neural networks are not and have saddles and plateaus.
4. SGD/minibatch gradients are unbiased but noisy (the noise helps escape saddles); momentum averages past gradients to damp oscillations; Adam adds per-parameter step sizes and is the practical default.
5. Minimising training loss is not the goal: expected test error = bias² + variance + noise; regularisation trades a little bias for a large reduction in variance.
6. Ridge (L2 disc) shrinks all weights and fixes ill-conditioning; Lasso (L1 diamond with corners on the axes) sets weights exactly to zero; standardise first and tune $\lambda$ by (grouped) cross-validation, never on the test set.
7. The four leakage types are duplicate/near-duplicate, temporal, group/specimen and preprocessing leakage; crops, spectra or frames from one specimen are correlated, so the default CV for EM data with a `specimen_id` is `GroupKFold` (chronological splits for time series).
8. Every preprocessing step that learns from data (scaler, PCA, feature selection) must be fitted inside the CV fold via a `Pipeline`; supervised selection on all data can produce $R^2>0$ on pure noise.
9. The split must match the deployment claim ("what kind of new": new crop, image, specimen, session, microscope, material); a random split tests only interpolation between near-copies.
10. A linear coefficient is a conditional, unit-dependent statement ("other features fixed"): compare standardised coefficients with bootstrap spread, distrust collinear pairs (signs can flip, Lasso keeps one arbitrarily), and read it only after the data (E1) and process (E2) levels are documented.

## Week 5 — From images to features: descriptors & tree ensembles

1. Classical ML on EM data needs a designed feature table (rows = objects such as particles or atom columns, columns = descriptors); segmentation/detection hyperparameters and errors propagate into correlated descriptor errors.
2. Atom-column finding: smooth (matched filter) → local maxima with a minimum distance and threshold → 2-D Gaussian least-squares refinement; sub-pixel precision is limited by counts, and least squares implies a Gaussian noise model (Poisson likelihood at low dose).
3. Good local-environment descriptors are translation, rotation and permutation invariant, continuous, contrast-sensitive and invariant to image-level nuisances (use ratios such as $I_\text{rel}=I_i/\mathrm{median}_j I_j$); ACSF/SOAP achieve invariance by summing over neighbours and using distances, angles or power spectra.
4. A decision tree greedily chooses the split maximising the impurity decrease $\Delta = I(\text{parent})-\frac{N_L}{N}I(L)-\frac{N_R}{N}I(R)$ (Gini/entropy for classification, variance for regression); trees need no feature scaling, capture thresholds/interactions, have high variance and cannot extrapolate.
5. Averaging $B$ predictors with variance $\sigma^2$ and correlation $\rho$ gives variance $\rho\sigma^2+\frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2$; random forests lower $\rho$ by bootstrap samples and random feature subsets at each split.
6. Gradient boosting is gradient descent in function space: fit a small tree to the pseudo-residuals $-\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\hat f$, update $\hat f\leftarrow\hat f+\eta h_t$; small $\eta$ plus early stopping on grouped validation folds controls overfitting.
7. Tree ensembles are the default first model for heterogeneous tabular data (robust to uninformative features, non-smooth targets, meaningful axes), but the baseline ladder (majority → linear → RF/GBM → NN) evaluated on identical grouped folds decides — with good physical descriptors a linear model can be as good.
8. Object-level EM data must be cross-validated by the unit that shares nuisances (image, session, specimen); features that identify the image (background, absolute intensity) inflate random-split scores.
9. Impurity (MDI) importance is computed on training data and biased towards continuous/high-cardinality features (noise gets non-zero credit); permutation importance on held-out groups is honest but correlated features mask each other and single-feature permutations of dependent features create off-manifold inputs → permute correlated groups together.
10. SHAP decomposes one prediction as $f(\mathbf{x})=\phi_0+\sum_j\phi_j$ (completeness, signed Shapley values; exact and fast for trees via TreeSHAP); it explains the model, not physical causation, and depends on the background/baseline choice.

## Week 6 — Neural networks & backpropagation

1. **Dense layer and depth:** a layer computes $\mathbf{a} = \sigma(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b})$ with $M(D+1)$ parameters; stacking purely linear layers collapses to one affine map $(\mathbf{W}^{(2)}\mathbf{W}^{(1)})\mathbf{x} + \tilde{\mathbf{b}}$ — only nonlinear activations (ReLU by default) make depth meaningful; a single neuron draws one hyperplane and cannot solve XOR.
2. **Output activation and loss are matched:** identity + MSE (regression), sigmoid + binary cross-entropy, softmax + categorical cross-entropy; softmax + CE gives the output gradient $\hat{\mathbf{p}} - \mathbf{y}$.
3. **Backprop = reverse-mode automatic differentiation:** the forward pass stores $\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}, \mathbf{a}^{(\ell)}$; the backward pass runs the delta recursion $\boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell)} = \sigma'(\mathbf{z}^{(\ell)}) \odot \mathbf{W}^{(\ell+1)\top}\boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell+1)}$ with $\nabla_{\mathbf{W}^{(\ell)}}\mathcal{L} = \boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell)}\mathbf{a}^{(\ell-1)\top}$ and $\nabla_{\mathbf{b}^{(\ell)}}\mathcal{L} = \boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell)}$. You must be able to do this by hand for a 2-layer ReLU network (an inactive ReLU unit passes zero gradient).
4. **Cost:** backprop gives exact gradients for all $W$ parameters at $O(W)$ cost (≈ one extra pass); finite differences cost $O(W^2)$ and are approximate. Reverse mode wins because training maps many parameters to one scalar loss; memory for stored activations is the price.
5. **Gradient checking:** compare autograd/manual gradients with central differences $[\mathcal{L}(\theta+\epsilon\mathbf{e}_j) - \mathcal{L}(\theta-\epsilon\mathbf{e}_j)]/2\epsilon$ via the relative error $\|\mathbf{g}_a - \mathbf{g}_n\|/(\|\mathbf{g}_a\| + \|\mathbf{g}_n\|)$, in float64, without dropout; $< 10^{-7}$ is fine, $> 10^{-2}$ is a bug.
6. **Vanishing/exploding gradients:** the backward pass multiplies one factor $\mathrm{diag}(\sigma')\mathbf{W}^\top$ per layer; sigmoid ($\sigma' \le 0.25$) shrinks gradients as $0.25^L$, large weights make them explode (→ NaN). Fixes: ReLU, correct initialisation, normalisation, gradient clipping, residual connections (Week 7).
7. **Initialisation:** zero/constant init never breaks symmetry (all hidden units stay identical). Choose the scale so the activation variance stays constant: $\mathrm{Var}(z) = D_\text{in}\mathrm{Var}(w)\mathbb{E}[a^2]$ ⇒ Xavier $\mathrm{Var}(w) = 2/(D_\text{in}+D_\text{out})$ for tanh/sigmoid, He $\mathrm{Var}(w) = 2/D_\text{in}$ for ReLU.
8. **Normalisation:** standardise inputs (fit on the training split only). BatchNorm normalises each unit over the mini-batch (+ learnable $\gamma,\beta$), uses running statistics at inference (`model.eval()`), and degrades with the small batches typical for large EM images; LayerNorm/GroupNorm normalise within one sample and are batch-size independent.
9. **Training diagnostics:** always plot train and validation loss (log scale) and recognise LR-too-high, LR-too-low, healthy and overfitting patterns; pick $\eta$ with an LR range test (steepest region, 3–10× below the minimum); sanity-check the initial loss ($\approx \ln K$ for $K$ classes) and that the network can overfit one small batch — if not, it is a bug.
10. **Baseline ladder:** mean → linear → RF/GBM → NN, all on the same `GroupKFold` split; on small tabular/descriptor data tree ensembles or a model with the correct physics feature often win (notebook: Ridge on $1/\sqrt{d}$ $R^2 \approx 0.91$, GBM $0.88$, small MLP $0.65$) — a neural network must earn its place.

## Week 7 — CNNs & U-Nets for microscopy

1. A convolutional layer is a dense layer constrained by **locality** and **weight sharing**: it has $C_{out}(C_{in}k^2+1)$ parameters, independent of the image size, and output size $H_{out}=\lfloor (H+2p-k)/s\rfloor+1$.
2. Convolution is **translation-equivariant** ($f(Tx)=Tf(x)$); **invariance** ($f(Tx)=f(x)$) is created by pooling / global average pooling. Segmentation needs equivariance, image-level classification may use invariance.
3. Plain CNNs are **not rotation- or mirror-equivariant**; rotation robustness comes from augmentation, test-time averaging over the symmetry group, or group-equivariant layers.
4. Stacking $3\times3$ convolutions grows the receptive field by 2 pixels per layer with fewer parameters and more non-linearities than one large kernel (VGG design); pooling/stride multiplies the growth.
5. ResNet blocks learn a residual, $\mathbf{y}=F(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{x}$: the identity path makes very deep networks trainable (degradation problem), with BatchNorm inside the blocks.
6. U-Net = encoder (context) + decoder (resolution) + **concatenating skip connections** (localisation) → one class score per pixel, trained with per-pixel cross-entropy (often + Dice).
7. Evaluate segmentation with **IoU** $=|A\cap B|/|A\cup B|$ and **Dice** $=2|A\cap B|/(|A|+|B|)=2\mathrm{IoU}/(1+\mathrm{IoU})$; pixel accuracy is misleading under class imbalance (predicting nothing on 9% foreground gives 91% accuracy).
8. Class imbalance is handled in the loss (soft Dice, weighted BCE, focal loss) and in sampling; validate segmenters by micrograph/specimen/session, never by overlapping tiles of the same image.
9. Gradient saliency $|\partial z_c/\partial\mathbf{x}|$ (pixel-level sensitivity), Grad-CAM (gradient-weighted last-conv feature maps, coarse regions) and occlusion (model-agnostic probability drop) are local explanations for CNNs.
10. Explanations diagnose **shortcut learning** that same-distribution test accuracy cannot reveal (shortcut model: 1.00 in-distribution, 0.50 in deployment); they must pass a weight-randomisation sanity check and be confirmed by interventions (occlusion, shifted test sets, retraining).

## Week 8 — Small data: augmentation, transfer & self-supervision

1. In EM the bottleneck is expert labels, not raw data: with ~50 labelled images and millions of parameters a network memorises shortcuts (vignetting, session contrast, scale bar); diagnose with the train–val gap, Grad-CAM and specimen-grouped validation — always **split by specimen first, then augment** (training set only).
2. Every augmentation is a claim that the physics has that symmetry; it must be applied jointly to image and mask. Illegal examples: rotating an EBSD/IPF map, vertically flipping a directionally solidified or welded structure, intensity jitter on quantitative EELS/Z-contrast, and zoom/rescale when grain size or lattice spacing is the label.
3. Transfer learning = keep the pretrained backbone, replace the head. Few labels / small domain gap → feature extraction (frozen backbone); more labels / large gap → fine-tuning with differential learning rates ($\eta_\text{backbone}\approx 10^{-5}\ll\eta_\text{head}\approx 10^{-3}$), gradual unfreezing top-down, or LP→FT, to avoid catastrophic forgetting. Replicate grey images to 3 channels rather than re-initialising the first conv layer; mind BatchNorm statistics.
4. Synthetic data gives perfect labels by construction (label → image); it transfers when the generator captures the task-relevant invariant (e.g. grain topology) and spans the imaging-condition nuisance distribution (dose, defocus, aberrations — "simulate the right variation, not more structures"); it fails on whatever the generator omits (e.g. twins). Close the gap with realistic noise, augmentation and a few real labels before trying CycleGAN/adversarial adaptation.
5. Self-supervised learning trains a backbone on a pretext task whose labels come from the data itself, so it can use the unlabelled in-domain images (thousands of frames, 4D-STEM patterns) that EM produces in abundance.
6. SimCLR: two augmented views per image, encoder $f$ + projector $g$, InfoNCE/NT-Xent loss $\ell_{i,j}=-\log\frac{\exp(\mathrm{sim}(\mathbf{z}_i,\mathbf{z}_j)/\tau)}{\sum_{k\neq i}\exp(\mathrm{sim}(\mathbf{z}_i,\mathbf{z}_k)/\tau)}$ with cosine similarity and temperature $\tau$; it is a $(2N-1)$-way softmax classification with chance level $\log(2N-1)$; keep $f$, discard $g$. The augmentations define the invariances — i.e. they are the labels.
7. Representation collapse (all inputs → same vector) is prevented by negatives (SimCLR), a reconstruction target (MAE: mask ~75 % of patches, reconstruct them; strong after fine-tuning, weaker as frozen features), or an EMA teacher with centring and sharpening (DINO/BYOL; no negatives; DINOv2 frozen features are a strong generic baseline).
8. Foundation models (DINOv2, CLIP, SAM) are pretrained once on broad data and adapted by prompting, probing or fine-tuning; SAM is a promptable segmenter (heavy encoder once, light decoder per click) and a strong annotation accelerator, but zero-shot performance on EM is mediocre — μSAM shows microscopy fine-tuning is needed. Always report zero-shot and fine-tuned results side by side.
9. Protocols: linear probe (logistic regression on frozen embeddings — classifier *and* measurement of embedding quality; best with very few labels) vs fine-tuning / LP→FT (wins with more labels) vs from scratch (needs many labels). State the protocol and $N$ with every number and compare against a random-init backbone.
10. Embedding retrieval: L2-normalise embeddings, rank by cosine similarity, evaluate with precision@k; use it to find similar micrographs, de-duplicate before splitting, choose diverse images to label and flag out-of-distribution frames. A pretty 2-D map is not evidence — colour it by nuisance (session, detector) and trust the probe.

## Week 9 — Unsupervised learning, autoencoders & latent spaces

1. k-means minimises the within-cluster sum of squares $\sum_k\sum_{i\in C_k}\|\mathbf{x}_i-\boldsymbol\mu_k\|^2$ by alternating assignment and centroid update (Lloyd); it converges only to a local minimum, so use k-means++ initialisation and several restarts. It assumes spherical, similar-sized clusters and is dominated by high-intensity features unless spectra are normalised or compressed first.
2. A Gaussian mixture model is fitted by EM: the E-step computes responsibilities $\gamma_{ik}=\pi_k\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i;\boldsymbol\mu_k,\Sigma_k)/\sum_j\pi_j\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i;\boldsymbol\mu_j,\Sigma_j)$; the M-step sets $\boldsymbol\mu_k,\Sigma_k,\pi_k$ to responsibility-weighted means, covariances and fractions. Each iteration never decreases the log-likelihood; k-means is the hard-assignment limit.
3. Because a GMM has a likelihood, the number of components can be chosen with BIC $=-2\log p(\mathcal D;\hat\theta_K)+p_K\log N$; k-means needs heuristics (elbow, silhouette).
4. An autoencoder learns an encoder $f_\phi$ and decoder $g_\theta$ by minimising reconstruction error through a bottleneck; a linear AE with MSE loss spans the PCA subspace, so nonlinearity is what lets an AE follow curved spectral manifolds. A denoising AE maps corrupted inputs to clean targets and learns noise-robust features.
5. A VAE's encoder outputs $q_\phi(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol\mu,\operatorname{diag}\boldsymbol\sigma^2)$ and training maximises the ELBO $\mathbb{E}_q[\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})]-\mathrm{KL}(q_\phi(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})\,\|\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},I))\le\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x})$ (Jensen); the gap equals the KL between $q_\phi$ and the true posterior. The reconstruction term is the negative log-likelihood of the noise model (Gaussian → MSE, Poisson → Poisson NLL).
6. The reparameterisation trick $\mathbf{z}=\boldsymbol\mu_\phi(\mathbf{x})+\boldsymbol\sigma_\phi(\mathbf{x})\odot\boldsymbol\epsilon$, $\boldsymbol\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},I)$, makes sampling differentiable so the VAE can be trained by backpropagation; the Gaussian KL is closed form, $\tfrac12\sum_j(\mu_j^2+\sigma_j^2-\log\sigma_j^2-1)$.
7. The KL weight β (β-VAE) trades reconstruction against latent regularity; too much prior pressure causes posterior collapse (KL → 0, decoder ignores $\mathbf{z}$), and unused latent dimensions switch off. The number of active dimensions depends on β and the decoder variance and is not a measurement of physical degrees of freedom.
8. A rotationally invariant VAE (rVAE) uses a coordinate-based decoder in which rotation and translation latents act on the pixel grid, so the content latents of atomic-resolution patches are free of orientation — invariance built into the architecture.
9. t-SNE/UMAP maps are for seeing, not measuring: inter-cluster distances, cluster sizes and positions depend on perplexity/n_neighbors and seed. Cluster and measure in the latent space, keep only structures that survive a hyperparameter/seed sweep, and require a linear probe (with a held-out specimen split) before claiming that a latent axis "is" a physical variable.
10. A high GMM responsibility or a clean latent map is not physical truth: mixed interface spectra can be assigned confidently to an intermediate phase, and a latent map may track a nuisance variable (thickness, dose, drift). Validate latent clusters and maps against reference spectra, spatial context and physical observables.

## Week 10 — Attention, transformers & graph networks for EM

1. **Scaled dot-product attention:** $\mathrm{Attention}(Q,K,V)=\mathrm{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d_k})\,V$ with $Q=XW_Q$, $K=XW_K$, $V=XW_V$ and tokens as rows of $X\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d}$; the attention matrix $A$ is $L\times L$ with rows summing to 1, and each output is a content-weighted average of the values; compute and memory scale as $\mathcal{O}(L^2)$, so the tokenisation sets the cost.
2. **Why $1/\sqrt{d_k}$:** for unit-variance entries $\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{q}^\top\mathbf{k})=d_k$; without the scaling, large scores saturate the softmax (nearly one-hot), its gradient vanishes and training stalls.
3. **Multi-head attention:** $H$ heads of width $d/H$ run in parallel, are concatenated and projected by $W_O$; each head learns its own similarity at the cost of one full-width head. Heads do not have guaranteed interpretable roles.
4. **Permutation equivariance:** $\mathrm{Attention}(PX)=P\,\mathrm{Attention}(X)$, so attention sees a set. Positional encodings (sinusoidal, learned, RoPE) are added to the tokens to restore order, and choosing one is a physics decision: 2-D for patterns, absolute energy for spectra, causal for time series. Ablating the encoding tests whether the model uses geometry.
5. **Transformer block and ViT:** each block is $X + \mathrm{MHA}(\mathrm{LN}(X))$ followed by $X + \mathrm{MLP}(\mathrm{LN}(X))$. A ViT splits the image into patches, embeds each linearly, adds a [CLS] token and positional embeddings, stacks blocks and classifies from [CLS]. Its weak inductive bias makes it data-hungry, so on EM data it needs pretraining (masked autoencoder: mask ~75 % of patches and reconstruct them, no labels needed) or a CNN baseline.
6. **Tokenising EM data is the inductive bias:** image patches (discs can straddle seams), one token per Bragg disc (e.g. $(k_r,k_\theta,I)$, as in 4D-STEM orientation/phase transformers with amortised inference ~100× faster than template matching), spectral windows along the energy axis, or 4D-STEM scan positions. Generative transformers (DiffractGPT) map a diffraction pattern to a structure token sequence, and their outputs are candidates that still need physics validation.
7. **Attention ≠ explanation:** attention weights ignore the magnitude of the values, the residual path that bypasses attention, and the mixing of tokens across layers, and different attention patterns can give the same prediction. Use attention maps to form hypotheses and spot shortcuts, then test them with an intervention (occlusion, masking, counterfactual). In the Week 10 notebook, attention and occlusion had rank correlation ≈ 0.
8. **Message passing:** $\mathbf{h}_i^{(\ell+1)}=U\big(\mathbf{h}_i^{(\ell)},\bigoplus_{j\in\mathcal{N}(i)}M(\mathbf{h}_i^{(\ell)},\mathbf{h}_j^{(\ell)},\mathbf{e}_{ij})\big)$ on an atom-column graph (nodes = columns with intensity features; edges within a cutoff $r_c$ with distance or displacement features, which carry strain). With a symmetric aggregation (sum, mean or max), the layer is permutation-equivariant, and a sum or mean readout makes the output permutation-invariant.
9. **GNN depth:** after $L$ layers a node has seen its $L$-hop neighbourhood (its receptive field). Too few layers cannot capture the label's interaction range, while too many cause over-smoothing. Use relative displacements, not absolute positions, to keep translation invariance, and split by image or specimen, never by node.
10. **Unifying picture:** CNN = message passing on a grid graph with fixed, offset-dependent weights; GNN = sparse graph from physics; transformer = complete graph with input-dependent weights $\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{q}_i^\top\mathbf{k}_j/\sqrt{d_k})$; RNN = causal chain. Choosing an architecture means choosing which inputs interact and how much structure is hard-coded versus learned.

## Week 11 — Uncertainty, Gaussian processes & autonomous EM

1. **Aleatoric vs epistemic uncertainty:** aleatoric = irreducible noise (shot noise, grain-to-grain scatter); epistemic = reducible lack of knowledge (few measurements, unseen instrument/specimen). Diagnostic: "Would more data of the same kind reduce it?" Yes → epistemic, No → aleatoric. Law of total variance: $\mathrm{Var}[y_*] = \mathbb{E}_\theta[\sigma^2_\epsilon] + \mathrm{Var}_\theta[\mu]$ (aleatoric + epistemic).
2. **Deep ensembles & MC dropout:** ensembles train $M\approx5$ networks from different seeds, and their disagreement is the epistemic signal (best empirical calibration, $M\times$ training cost). MC dropout keeps dropout on at inference and runs $T\approx30$ passes (no extra training; `model.eval()` switches dropout off). For segmentation, use the per-pixel predictive entropy $H_i=-\sum_c \bar p_{i,c}\log\bar p_{i,c}$ with a reject-for-review threshold $\tau$, and report the full operating curve (review rate vs recall). Example: MetalDAM auto recall 82.9%, rising by +3.5 points at 5% review. Confidently wrong pixels are never flagged.
3. **Calibration:** a reliability diagram plots predicted confidence against observed accuracy (diagonal = calibrated, below = overconfident). $\mathrm{ECE}=\sum_b \frac{|\mathcal B_b|}{N}|\mathrm{acc}(\mathcal B_b)-\mathrm{conf}(\mathcal B_b)|$. Temperature scaling $\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf z/T)$ fixes confidences post hoc without changing the argmax or the accuracy. Recalibrate on a held-out, lab-realistic set after any detector, gun or coating change.
4. **Split conformal prediction:** fit any predictor on the training set and compute scores $s_i=|y_i-\hat f(\mathbf x_i)|$ on a separate calibration set. Take $\hat q$ = the $\lceil(n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil$-th smallest score and output $[\hat f\pm\hat q]$. This guarantees $\Pr(y_*\in C)\ge 1-\alpha$ for any model, provided the data are *exchangeable*, and the coverage is marginal. Using training residuals instead undercovers (notebook: 0.78 instead of the 0.90 target; split conformal reaches 0.93). **CQR** conformalises lower and upper quantile regressors, so the width adapts to heteroscedastic noise with the same guarantee. Under distribution shift the guarantee is lost (notebook: coverage falls to 0.70).
5. **GP definition and posterior:** $f\sim\mathcal{GP}(m,k)$, so any finite set of function values is jointly Gaussian. The posterior mean is $\mu_*=\mathbf k_*^\top(\mathbf K+\sigma_n^2\mathbf I)^{-1}\mathbf y$ and the posterior variance is $\sigma_*^2=k(\mathbf x_*,\mathbf x_*)-\mathbf k_*^\top(\mathbf K+\sigma_n^2\mathbf I)^{-1}\mathbf k_*$. Both are exact and cost $O(N^3)$. The variance depends only on where we measured, not on the $y$ values.
6. **Uncertainty balloons and kernels:** far from the data $\mathbf k_*\to\mathbf 0$, so $\sigma_*^2\to\sigma_f^2$. Notebook (SEED=42): σ* = 0.0145 near the data vs 0.1966 far away (13.5×), and one extra point cuts σ* at the far location by 49%. RBF length-scale ℓ: a short ℓ gives a wiggly mean, while a long ℓ gives falsely confident extrapolation. The hyperparameters are chosen by maximising the log marginal likelihood (data fit − complexity = automatic Occam's razor). Matérn-5/2 is often a better default for materials data.
7. **Bayesian optimisation loop:** initialise → fit GP → maximise the acquisition function → measure (the expensive step) → update → repeat. Report the best *observed* point. Acquisition functions: UCB $\mu_*+\kappa\sigma_*$ ($\kappa$ = exploration weight), EI $(\mu_*-f^+-\xi)\Phi(Z)+\sigma_*\phi(Z)$, and the greedier PI $\Phi(Z)$. Notebook: UCB κ=3 best 0.9323 vs random 0.7229 on the same 15-measurement budget. With κ ≤ 2 the search gets stuck at the deceptive local optimum (0.744), so the amount of exploration matters more than the choice of acquisition function.
8. **Deep kernel learning and automated 4D-STEM:** $k_\text{DKL}(\mathbf x,\mathbf x')=k_\text{RBF}(g(\mathbf x;\mathbf w),g(\mathbf x';\mathbf w))$, with the network weights and GP hyperparameters trained jointly on the marginal likelihood. This allows BO/active learning on image-patch inputs: a HAADF patch is the input, a scalarised diffraction quantity (CoM, strain) is the target, and the acquisition picks the next probe position. Result: maps from a small fraction of positions, i.e. large dose savings for beam-sensitive specimens (Roccapriore et al. 2022). DKL needs enough data to learn $g$.
9. **OOD detection as a trust gate:** calibration ≠ OOD detection. Softmax confidence, ensemble agreement and GP σ can all be silently wrong under shift (new detector, dose damage, new alloy family). OOD scores: Mahalanobis distance of feature embeddings, ensemble disagreement, autoencoder reconstruction error, latent nearest-neighbour distance. Set the threshold at the 95th percentile of in-distribution scores. Act automatically only if σ is small **and** the OOD score is low; otherwise refuse and escalate to a human. Log every decision.
10. **Automation time scales and RL outlook:** BO for slow loops (minutes to hours per measurement), RL policies $\pi(a\mid s)$ maximising $\mathbb E[\sum_t\gamma^t r_t]$ for fast control (autofocus, alignment), with DKL in between. Risks: reward hacking, the sim-to-real gap, and selection bias. Always archive where the agent measured and where it did not.

## Week 12 — Inverse problems I: regularisation, tomography & sensor fusion

1. Every reconstruction starts from a forward model $\mathbf{y} = H\mathbf{x} + \boldsymbol\epsilon$: $H$ comes from the physics (PSF, Radon projector, $Z^\gamma$ HAADF model), the data term from the noise model (Gaussian → squared error, Poisson → Poisson NLL).
2. Hadamard: a problem is well-posed only if a solution exists, is unique and depends stably on the data; EM inverse problems typically violate uniqueness (limited angles) and stability (small singular values).
3. The condition number $\kappa = \sigma_\text{max}/\sigma_\text{min}$ bounds noise amplification; the naive inverse divides by tiny singular values (notebook: $\kappa \approx 230$, naive RMSE 0.968 vs Tikhonov 0.140).
4. Tikhonov: $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = (H^\top H + \lambda L^\top L)^{-1} H^\top \mathbf{y}$; in the SVD picture it multiplies each component by $\sigma_i^2/(\sigma_i^2+\lambda)$. Regulariser = −log prior (MAP): L2 ↔ smooth, TV ↔ piecewise constant (edges), L1 ↔ sparse, plus $\mathbf{x} \geq 0$.
5. The error is U-shaped in $\lambda$; without ground truth choose $\lambda$ with the L-curve corner, the discrepancy principle ($\|H\hat{\mathbf{x}} - \mathbf{y}\| \approx \|\boldsymbol\epsilon\|$) or cross-validation, and always report it.
6. TV is non-differentiable and is solved by proximal splitting / ADMM: alternate a data-consistency step and a prior (denoising, soft-threshold) step; plug-and-play replaces that prior step by a learned denoiser.
7. Radon transform = line integrals $p_\theta(s)$ of the object; the sinogram stacks them. Fourier-slice theorem: the 1-D FT of a projection equals the central slice of the object's 2-D FT at the same angle.
8. FBP = ramp filter $|k|$ + back-projection; exact only for complete, noise-free data. SART is iterative least squares (+ non-negativity); SART + TV adds a prior step (notebook, ±60°: FBP 0.218, SART 0.111, SART + TV 0.088 RMSE).
9. The missing wedge is a null space of the projector: reconstructions elongate along the beam, and priors can only fill it with plausible values, never measure it.
10. HAADF + EDS fusion: $\arg\min_{\mathbf{x}_i\ge0} \tfrac12\|\mathbf{b}_H - \sum_i (Z_i\mathbf{x}_i)^\gamma\|^2 + \lambda_1\sum_i(\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{b}_i^\top\log\mathbf{x}_i) + \lambda_2\sum_i\|\mathbf{x}_i\|_\text{TV}$ — HAADF fixes *where*, EDS fixes *what*; ≈ 10× dose reduction, but over-smoothing at large TV weight.

## Week 13 — Inverse problems II: ptychography, generative priors & synthesis

1. **Phase problem & ptychographic redundancy:** detectors record $I=|\psi|^2$, so the phase — which carries almost all structural information of a thin specimen — is lost. Ptychography scans an overlapping probe; each object pixel appears in many diffraction patterns $I_j = |\mathcal{F}[P(\mathbf{r})\,O(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_j)]|^2$, making phase retrieval over-determined. More overlap → lower error (notebook: step 4/6/8 px → 0.0021/0.0067/0.0091 amplitude error after 40 ePIE iterations).
2. **ePIE:** at each position (1) predict $\Psi_j=\mathcal{F}[P\cdot O_j]$, (2) replace $|\Psi_j|$ by $\sqrt{I_j}$ keeping the phase, (3) back-transform to $\psi'_j$, (4) update $O_j \leftarrow O_j + \beta\,\frac{P^*}{|P|^2_{\max}}(\psi'_j-\psi_j)$; loop over shuffled positions. Monitor the amplitude-consistency error (no ground truth needed); low error means self-consistent, not necessarily correct. [@maiden2009improved]
3. **Object models:** weak-phase approximation $O\approx 1+i\sigma V_p$ linearises the forward model; the strong-phase approximation keeps $\psi_{\text{exit}}=\psi_0 e^{i\sigma V_p}$ but assumes the probe does not change inside the sample. It fails for thick specimens (≳10–20 nm), low beam energy and strong scatterers → **multislice**: transmit through thin slices and Fresnel-propagate between them, $\psi_{n+1}=p(\Delta z)\otimes[\psi_n e^{i\sigma V_n}]$. Depth information has a missing wedge; thermal vibrations set the ultimate resolution limit (~20 pm).
4. **Ptychography as gradient descent:** minimise $\sum_j \|\,|\mathcal{F}[P\cdot O_j]|-\sqrt{I_j}\,\|^2 + \lambda R$ (amplitude loss ≈ variance-stabilised Poisson). Automatic differentiation computes the gradient through FFT and modulus, so probe, positions, slices, tilt angles and priors are just extra parameters/terms. ePIE ≈ SGD with batch size 1 and a hand-derived step. Notebook: autodiff Adam reaches $8.7\times10^{-5}$ vs $7.7\times10^{-4}$ (ePIE) amplitude error after 200 passes.
5. **Prior strength vs dose:** at low dose (100 e⁻/pattern) a TV prior improves the phase error (0.103 → 0.080 rad at the best $\lambda_{TV}$) but too much TV biases it (0.140 rad) — a U-curve. Early stopping acts as an implicit regulariser. The weaker the data, the more the prior decides the result.
6. **GAN → diffusion:** GANs play $\min_G\max_D$ and give sharp one-step samples but train unstably, mode-collapse and give no likelihood. Diffusion models add noise with $q(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{x}_0)=\mathcal{N}(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\mathbf{x}_0,(1-\bar\alpha_t)\mathbf{I})$ and train a denoiser with $\|\boldsymbol\epsilon-\boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,t)\|^2$: stable, diverse, slow to sample, and they provide the score $\nabla\log p_t(\mathbf{x}_t)$.
7. **Diffusion posterior sampling:** posterior score = prior score + likelihood score, $\nabla\log p_t(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{y})=\nabla\log p_t(\mathbf{x}_t)+\nabla\log p_t(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_t)$. DPS approximates the likelihood at the Tweedie estimate $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0=(\mathbf{x}_t-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ and adds $-\zeta\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t}\|\mathbf{y}-H(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0)\|$ each step; works for non-linear $H$. Repeating with new seeds gives an ensemble; null-space directions are decided by the prior alone. [@chung2023dps]
8. **Physics constraints:** soft = penalty $\lambda\|\mathcal{N}[f_\theta]\|^2$ in the loss (PINN; needs λ, approximately satisfied); hard = built into parameterisation/architecture ($O=e^{i\phi}$, softplus for positivity, Lagaris substitution for boundary conditions, equivariant networks). Hard constraints are exact but only as good as their physics; wrong physics biases the result confidently.
9. **Trusting a generative reconstruction:** red flags — very low dose with a noise-free result, features absent from training data, structured residuals, headline result. Checks — normalised residuals $\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$, ensemble agreement, comparison with a prior-free (ePIE/TV) reconstruction, higher-dose or independent validation. Distribution shift (train ≠ specimen) is the most common cause of hallucination. Correlation ≠ causation: only interventions establish causes.
10. **Course synthesis:** E1–E6 explainability ladder (data, process, feature, model, prediction, decision) collected across the course (W2/W4 data, W2/W12–13 process, W4–W5 features, W6–W10 model, W7/W11 prediction, W11/W13 decision). Recurring trust failures — leakage (W4), shortcut learning (W7), miscalibration/OOD (W11), hallucination (W13) — share one root cause: a statistical association or prior replaced the physical signal. Five pillars: correct physics, honest validation, calibrated confidence, faithful explanation, expert validation.
