Today’s red line: from an image to a classifier you can explain

  • This is the map of the lecture. Six stations, each one question. The rail at the top right of every slide shows where we are.
  • 1 Objects: find the atom columns. Smooth, find local maxima, refine each maximum with a 2-D Gaussian fit. The precision of a position is set by the dose.
  • 2 Descriptors: describe each object with a few numbers. One row per object. Good descriptors do not change when the image is shifted, rotated or recorded with a different gain.
  • 3 Tree: a decision tree asks yes/no questions about the descriptors. It chooses each question greedily by the decrease of the impurity. A deep tree has a high variance.

One specimen, four images, one question

Four HAADF-STEM images of one cross-section: an oxide film on a LaAlO₃ substrate, with an amorphous layer on top. Different sessions, magnifications and doses.

The question for every intensity maximum: is it a substrate column, a film column, or no column at all?

  • The data: STEM-Bank-26 (Hollyer and Stach), folder “haadf_SFO_LAO”, licence CC BY 4.0. The file names say SFO on LAO. In HAADF the intensity grows with the atomic number. The bright square lattice on the left is the LaAlO₃ substrate (La, Z = 57). The darker lattice in the middle is the Sr–Fe oxide film. On the right is an amorphous layer. The metadata do not state this; it is my reading of the Z-contrast.
  • The four images. A: one frame, 2048², 4.7 pm per pixel. B: one frame, 1024², 9.8 pm per pixel. C: sum of 10 frames, 512², 15.5 pm per pixel. D: sum of 10 frames, 1024², 10 pm per pixel. The fields of view are 8 to 10 nm.

Where are the objects in the image?

From an image to objects: smooth, find maxima, fit

Image B, a strip across the interface. From left to right: the raw image; smoothed, with the slow background removed; the local maxima; the fitted positions, coloured by the label.

Three steps, three hyperparameters: the smoothing width, the minimum distance, the threshold. Each of them changes the table.

  • Purpose: the classical column finder in one picture. This is the workflow of Atomap and of most in-house scripts.
  • Step 1, smooth. A Gaussian filter with σ = 0.04 nm, about the size of one column. This is a matched filter (Week 2): the filter with the shape of the signal gives the best signal-to-noise ratio. Then subtract a wide Gaussian (0.8 nm). This removes the slow background from thickness and from the amorphous layer.
  • Step 2, local maxima. A pixel is a candidate when it is the largest value in a window of ±0.12 nm, and when it is above a threshold. Here the threshold is 4 % of the brightest values. The window is the minimum distance between two columns.
  • Step 3, fit. A 2-D Gaussian plus a constant, fitted by least squares in a window of ±0.11 nm. The fit gives the position with sub-pixel precision, the peak height, the width and the local background.
  • Result on the four images: 2 266 candidates. 598, 692, 389 and 587 for images A to D.
  • Look at the right panel. In the amorphous layer the finder also returns maxima. A finder does not know what a column is; it returns every maximum above the threshold. These false detections are a class of our problem.
  • Misconception: “the finder has no parameters, it only finds the atoms.” The next slide shows how much the result depends on the three parameters.
  • The one point to land: an object detector is the first model in the chain. Its errors go into every later number.

Find the columns: dose, smoothing, threshold

  • Purpose: students see that detection is a trade-off, and that the dose decides how hard it is.
  • The data: ten aligned frames of image D, a strip of 5.8 nm × 5.1 nm across the interface. The slider “frames summed” adds 1 to 10 frames, so it changes the dose. The circles are the maxima above the threshold, coloured by the zone.
  • Demo, step 1: ten frames, default smoothing and threshold. Read the counts: 33 maxima in the substrate, 104 in the film, and 57 in the amorphous layer. The amorphous layer has no columns, so these 57 are false detections.
  • Demo, step 2: raise the threshold. Read the chart on the right: it shows the count in each zone against the threshold. The substrate line is flat; these columns are safe. The amorphous curve falls to zero near 21 %. But at 21 % the film curve is at 70: one third of the film columns is gone. No threshold removes the false detections and keeps the film.
  • Demo, step 3: go to one frame. The image is noisier. Now 64 false detections, and the amorphous curve reaches zero only near 27 %, where half of the film columns are gone. A lower dose makes the trade-off worse.
  • Demo, step 4: set the smoothing to its minimum. The number of maxima rises: noise pixels become maxima. Set it too high: neighbouring columns merge.
  • Ask: “Which errors are worse, missed columns or false columns?” It depends on the question. A vacancy count needs no false negatives. A strain map tolerates missed columns.
  • Today we keep a low threshold and let a classifier decide what is a column. That is the reason for the class “no column”.
  • The one point to land: the hyperparameters of the detector decide which rows the table has. Report them together with the result.
  • If you are late: do steps 1 and 3 only.

Refine each maximum: a 2-D Gaussian fit

1 Model of one column: \(I(\mathbf r)=A\,\exp\!\big(-\lvert\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0\rvert^2/2\sigma^2\big)+b\) in a small window around the maximum.

2 Least squares: \(\min_{A,\mathbf r_0,\sigma,b}\sum_{\text{pixels}}\big(I_\text{obs}-I(\mathbf r)\big)^2\). Non-linear in \(\mathbf r_0\) and \(\sigma\): Gauss–Newton, Week 4.

3 Output for each column: position \(\mathbf r_0\), peak height \(A\), width \(\sigma\), background \(b\), residual. The first descriptors.

4 Precision: \(\sigma_{r_0}\approx\sigma/\sqrt{N}\) for \(N\) detected electrons in the column. The pixel size does not appear.

from scipy.optimize import least_squares
def model(p, yy, xx):                      # p = (A, y0, x0, sigma, b)
    return p[0] * np.exp(-((yy - p[1])**2 + (xx - p[2])**2) / (2 * p[3]**2)) + p[4]
fit = least_squares(lambda p: (model(p, yy, xx) - window).ravel(), p_start)
  • Write the four lines on the board while the fragments appear.
  • Step 1: the Gaussian is a model of the probe-broadened column. It is not exact, but it is good near the maximum. The constant b takes the local background.
  • Step 2: this is a non-linear least-squares problem with five parameters. Week 4 gave the tools: Gauss–Newton or Levenberg–Marquardt. scipy.optimize.least_squares does it. The start values come from the maximum.
  • Least squares implies a Gaussian noise model with one σ (Week 4, Act 1). That is fine for many counts. At a few counts per pixel, use the Poisson likelihood.
  • Step 3: the fit turns an image patch into five physical numbers. They are the raw material for the descriptors of Act 2.
  • Step 4: the position of a peak of width σ, measured with N electrons, has a standard error of about σ/√N. This is the standard error of a mean. With σ = 50 pm and N = 100 electrons, the precision is 5 pm. The pixel size only needs to sample the peak (two or more pixels across σ).
  • When two columns are closer than about 3σ, fit them together with two Gaussians. Otherwise each fit pulls towards the neighbour.
  • Misconception: “the precision cannot be better than one pixel.” The next slide measures it.
  • Forward link: a neural network can replace steps 1 and 2 of the finder (Week 7). The Gaussian fit stays as the last step.

The dose sets the precision, not the pixel size

Left: the repeatability of a column position, measured on 219 substrate columns from two independent sums of image D. Right: a cut through one column, for one frame and for the mean of ten frames (shown with 2 × 2 binning, 20 pm), with the Gaussian fits.

Pixel size 10 pm. Precision with one frame: 12 pm. With five frames: 5.6 pm. Factor \(\sqrt5\): the precision follows \(1/\sqrt{\text{dose}}\).

  • Purpose: measure the precision on real data, without a ground truth.
  • The method: image D has ten frames. Sum n frames, and sum n other frames. Find the same 219 substrate columns in both sums. The two positions of one column differ only by noise. The standard deviation of the difference, divided by √2, is the precision of one measurement. This is a repeatability test; you can do it with every image stack.
  • The numbers, for one coordinate. One frame: pixel maximum 13.3 pm, centroid 12.9 pm, Gaussian fit 12.2 pm. Two frames: 8.8, 7.9, 7.4 pm. Five frames: 6.6, 5.9, 5.6 pm.
  • First result: from one frame to five frames the precision improves by a factor of 2.2. √5 = 2.24. The precision is limited by the counts.
  • Second result: the three estimators differ by only 10 to 15 %. The pixel maximum is taken after the smoothing, and the smoothing is a matched filter. So the maximum of the smoothed image is already a good estimate. The fit is the best of the three, and it also gives A, σ and b.
  • Third result: 5.6 pm is about half a pixel. Sub-pixel precision is normal. Strain maps and polarisation maps in ferroelectrics need 1 to 5 pm; they get it with more dose and with drift correction.
  • A limit of this test: it measures the random error only. A scan distortion that is the same in all frames does not show up.
  • The one point to land: precision is a property of the dose and of the estimator. Measure it with a split of your own frames.

The same idea for particles: segment, then measure

A synthetic particle image processed with scikit-image: smooth, Otsu threshold, binary mask, watershed to split touching particles, and one table row per particle (regionprops).

Object Detector Hyperparameters Typical descriptors
atom column smooth → maxima → Gaussian fit smoothing width, minimum distance, threshold position, height, width, neighbours
particle, grain, pore smooth → threshold → watershed smoothing width, threshold rule, marker distance area, equivalent diameter, eccentricity, solidity
  • Purpose: generalise. Atom columns are one kind of object. At lower magnification the objects are particles, grains, pores or precipitates, and the detector is a segmentation.
  • This figure is synthetic; it is the only synthetic figure of today. Walk from left to right. Smooth. The Otsu threshold separates two intensity populations. In the binary mask, particles that touch are one region. The distance transform and the watershed split most of them. skimage.measure.regionprops_table returns one row per region.
  • The descriptors: area, equivalent diameter √(4A/π), eccentricity of the fitted ellipse, solidity (area divided by the area of the convex hull), mean intensity. Convert pixels to nanometres before you model.
  • Error propagation. A merge of two particles changes the area, the eccentricity and the solidity at the same time. So the errors of the descriptors are correlated. They are not independent noise on each column of the table. Solidity is a good quality check: merged particles have a low solidity.
  • Other pitfalls. Particles at the image border are cut: remove them (clear_border). A 2-D projection is not the 3-D shape. Objects smaller than about 3 pixels are dominated by the probe and the noise.
  • The one point to land: the table is a designed representation. Each column of the table is a hypothesis about what matters, and each row carries the errors of the detector.
  • If you are late: show the table only and say one sentence about correlated errors.

Checkpoint: the detector

Two minutes, in pairs. Say what changes in the table.

  1. You double the smoothing width. → Fewer false maxima, but close columns merge. Rows disappear, and the fitted widths grow.
  2. You want 3 pm precision; one frame gives 12 pm. → 16 times the dose (\(1/\sqrt N\)), for example 16 aligned frames. A smaller pixel does not help.
  3. The specimen is beam-sensitive: 5 electrons per pixel. → Poisson likelihood in the fit, not least squares (Week 2).
  4. The watershed merges two particles. → Area, eccentricity and solidity are wrong together: correlated errors in one row.
  5. A colleague reports “412 columns found”. What do you ask? → Threshold, minimum distance, smoothing, and the number of false detections on a region without columns.
  • Procedure: show the five cases, give two minutes, then reveal the answers one by one. Ask one pair for each case.
  • Case 1: the smoothing width is a trade-off between noise and resolution. Too wide, and two columns at 0.14 nm become one maximum.
  • Case 2: the precision goes with 1/√N. A factor of 4 in precision needs a factor of 16 in dose. Students often answer “use a smaller pixel”. A smaller pixel at the same dose only divides the same electrons over more pixels.
  • Case 3: least squares assumes Gaussian noise. At 5 counts the distribution is skewed. This is the rule of Weeks 2 and 4: the noise model gives the loss.
  • Case 4: this is must-know statement 1. Detection errors become correlated descriptor errors.
  • Case 5: a count without the detector settings cannot be reproduced. The amorphous region of our images is a free test for false detections.
  • Timing: 3 minutes in total. If you are late, do cases 2 and 4 only.

Act 2 · Descriptors

Which numbers describe one object?

One row per object: the feature table

Descriptor Meaning Unit Kind
amp, bg peak height and background of the fit counts raw intensity
sigma width of the fit nm shape
resid RMS residual of the fit / peak height – shape
contrast peak height / background – ratio
I_rel peak height / median peak height of the 4 nearest – ratio
d1, d4 distance to the nearest; mean distance to the 4 nearest nm geometry
d_spread std / mean of the 4 distances – geometry
n_near number of candidates within 0.45 nm – geometry
psi4 four-fold order \(\big\lvert\tfrac14\sum_{j=1}^{4}e^{4i\theta_j}\big\rvert\) of the 4 nearest – geometry

1 934 rows × 11 columns. From here on the image is gone: the model sees only this table.

  • Purpose: define the table of today. Read it by groups, not by rows.
  • Group 1, raw intensity: amp and bg come directly from the Gaussian fit, in detector counts.
  • Group 2, shape: sigma is the width of the column in nanometres. resid says how well a Gaussian describes the patch. A noise maximum is not a Gaussian, so its residual is large.
  • Group 3, ratios: contrast divides the peak height by the local background. I_rel divides the peak height by the median peak height of the four nearest neighbours. A ratio removes the gain of the detector. Only I_rel also removes an offset; the demo on the next slide shows it.
  • Group 4, geometry: distances and angles to the neighbours. d1 is the distance to the nearest candidate. d4 is the mean distance to the four nearest. d_spread is the relative spread of these four distances: zero on a perfect square lattice. n_near counts the candidates within 0.45 nm. psi4 is the four-fold bond order: 1 when the four neighbours sit at 90° to each other, small when they sit at random angles.
  • These 11 numbers are a choice. Each one is a hypothesis: “the film has a different lattice spacing”, “noise maxima have no order”. This is the difference from deep learning, where the network learns the features (Week 7).
  • The feature sets of today. “Raw”: amp, bg, sigma. “Invariant”: I_rel, d1, d4, d_spread, n_near, psi4. “All”: the 11 together.
  • The one point to land: classical machine learning on images needs a designed table. Rows are objects, columns are descriptors.

Describe one column

  • Purpose: connect each descriptor to something visible in the image, and test the invariances by hand.
  • Demo, step 1: click a substrate column. Read the table: d4 is the lattice spacing, psi4 is near 1, d_spread is near 0. The lines show the four nearest neighbours.
  • Demo, step 2: click a film column, then a maximum in the amorphous layer. Ask before each click: “Which numbers will change?” In the amorphous layer the neighbours are at irregular distances and angles: psi4 falls and d_spread rises.
  • Demo, step 3: switch on “gain ×3 + offset”. This is a different detector setting. amp and bg change. contrast changes too, from 0.50 to 0.43: it divides by the background, and an offset changes the background. I_rel, the widths and all distances show “same”.
  • Ask: “Why does one ratio survive and the other not?” I_rel is a ratio of two peak heights. The fit removes the constant under each peak, so the offset cancels before the division.
  • Demo, step 4: switch on “rotate 90°”. Nothing changes: all 11 descriptors use distances, or angles between neighbours. An x coordinate would change. That is why the position is not a descriptor.
  • The large circle has a radius of 0.9 nm. It is the cutoff of the symmetry functions on a later slide: the local environment of the column.
  • Misconception: “the position in the image is a good feature, the film is always in the middle.” It is true in these four images, and wrong in the next image. A descriptor must describe the object, not the field of view.
  • The one point to land: a good descriptor changes when the physics changes, and does not change when the acquisition changes.

Raw intensities identify the image

Top: the peak height amp of the substrate columns, for each of the four images. Bottom: the relative intensity I_rel of the same columns.

The median peak height differs by a factor of 4.3 between the images. A model that splits on amp learns which image, not which column.

  • Purpose: show the nuisance in the data before any model sees it.
  • Top panel: the peak height of the substrate columns in detector counts. The same lattice, the same atoms. The median is 4.9·10⁵ in image A, 9.8·10⁵ in B, 2.1·10⁶ in C and 1.5·10⁶ in D. The reasons: the number of summed frames, the dwell time, the detector gain, the local thickness. None of them is a property of the column.
  • A threshold “amp > 10⁶ means substrate column” is right in one image and wrong in the next one.
  • Bottom panel: I_rel, the peak height divided by the median of the four nearest neighbours. The four distributions are on top of each other. The division removes every factor that the column shares with its neighbours.
  • This is the same idea as the standardisation in Week 4, but local, and inside one image. It needs no information from other images, so it cannot leak.
  • What I_rel loses: the absolute Z-contrast. Deep in the substrate and deep in the film, I_rel is near 1 in both. It only differs near the interface. So the geometry descriptors must do most of the work. This is honest: without a calibrated detector, the absolute intensity cannot be compared between images.
  • With a calibrated detector (intensity as a fraction of the incident beam) and a known thickness, the absolute intensity is a valid descriptor. That is quantitative STEM.
  • The one point to land: before you train, plot each feature for each image. A feature whose distribution identifies the image will be used to identify the image.

Build the invariances into the descriptor

The acquisition changes … The descriptor must be … How to get it
the field of view (shift) translation invariant use distances to neighbours, not positions
the scan direction (rotation) rotation invariant use distances and angles between neighbours
the order of the neighbours permutation invariant sum or average over the neighbours
gain, offset, dwell time intensity invariant use ratios to the local neighbours
the pixel size scale aware measure in nm, not in pixels

And two more: continuous (a small shift of an atom gives a small change) and sensitive (different physics gives different numbers).

  • Purpose: turn the last two slides into a design rule. This is must-know statement 3.
  • Read each row as “what can change without a change of the physics”, then “what must the descriptor do”.
  • Row 1: the same column in a shifted image must get the same row. So no absolute positions.
  • Row 2: the scan direction is arbitrary. Distances are invariant. Angles between two neighbours are invariant; the angle of one neighbour against the x axis is not. psi4 uses exp(4iθ) and takes the absolute value of the mean: a rotation of all neighbours changes only the phase.
  • Row 3: “neighbour 1” and “neighbour 2” have no fixed order. Sums, means, medians and sorted lists are invariant under a change of the order.
  • Row 4: a ratio removes the gain. To remove an offset too, both numbers of the ratio must be free of it: I_rel passes, contrast fails.
  • Row 5: all lengths in nanometres with the calibrated pixel size. Then images with different magnifications share one table. This is not an invariance; the lattice spacing is physics, so the descriptor must keep it.
  • Continuity: n_near is not continuous. A neighbour at 0.449 nm counts, one at 0.451 nm does not. A smooth cutoff is better; the symmetry functions on the next slides have one.
  • Sensitivity is the opposite demand. A constant is invariant under everything and useless. The art is to remove the nuisance and to keep the physics.
  • Where the invariance comes from in deep learning: from data augmentation or from the architecture (Weeks 7 and 8). Here it comes from the design.
  • The one point to land: decide what must not matter, and make the descriptor blind to it.

Order and distance separate lattice from noise

Image B. Left: each candidate coloured by the four-fold order psi4. Right: coloured by the distance to the nearest neighbour, d1.

  • Purpose: look at two geometry descriptors on the image, before a model uses them.
  • Left, psi4. In the substrate and in the film the four nearest neighbours form a square: psi4 is near 1. In the amorphous layer the maxima are at random places: psi4 is low. So psi4 separates “column” from “no column”.
  • Right, d1. In the substrate the nearest neighbour is at 0.37 nm: the finder sees one square sublattice, the heavy La columns, and 0.379 nm is the lattice parameter of LaAlO₃. In the film the median is 0.28 nm, close to 0.38/√2 = 0.27 nm. There the two cation sublattices have a similar atomic number, so the finder sees both. So d1 separates substrate from film.
  • The class medians. psi4: substrate 1.00, film 0.90, no column 0.49. The film is lower than the substrate because its four nearest neighbours are not always a clean square. d1: 0.37, 0.28 and 0.26 nm. d_spread: 0.02, 0.09 and 0.15.
  • Each descriptor answers one of the two questions. No single descriptor answers both. This is why we need a model that combines them, and a tree is the simplest one.
  • Look at the interface. There the neighbourhood of a column contains both lattices. The descriptors of these columns are between the two classes. Expect the errors of every model there.
  • psi4 is the 2-D relative of the Steinhardt bond-order parameters that are used to find crystalline regions in simulations.
  • The one point to land: plot the descriptor on the image. If you can see the classes in this plot, a simple model can find them.

From hand-made to systematic: symmetry functions

Radial symmetry functions (ACSF, \(G^2\)) Behler, Jörg et al., (2007), doi:10.1103/PhysRevLett.98.146401

\[G_i(r_s)=\sum_{j\ne i} e^{-(r_{ij}-r_s)^2/2w^2}\,f_c(r_{ij})\]

  • one number for each shell radius \(r_s\): “how many neighbours at this distance?”
  • a sum over neighbours: translation, rotation and permutation invariant
  • a smooth cutoff \(f_c\): continuous
  • angular functions and SOAP Bartók, Albert P. et al., (2013), doi:10.1103/PhysRevB.87.184115 add angles; library: DScribe Himanen, Lauri et al., (2020), doi:10.1016/j.cpc.2019.106949
  • Purpose: show that the hand-made descriptors have a systematic version. ACSF and SOAP come from machine-learned interatomic potentials. They work for atom columns in an image too.
  • The formula: put a Gaussian shell of radius r_s and width w around column i. Count the neighbours in the shell, with a smooth weight. Do this for several radii. The result is a radial fingerprint, like a smoothed radial distribution function of one column.
  • Invariances: the function uses only the distances r_ij, and it sums over all neighbours. So it is invariant by construction. The cutoff function goes smoothly to zero at 0.9 nm, so a neighbour that crosses the cutoff causes no jump.
  • The figure: the substrate has sharp shells at its lattice distances. The film has other shells. The noise maxima have a flat fingerprint: no preferred distance.
  • Result on our task (random forest, new image): I_rel plus the seven radial functions give 84.0 %. The six hand-made descriptors give 84.1 %. No gain here, because the hand-made set already contains the distances.
  • When are systematic descriptors better? When you do not know which distances and angles matter: defects, grain boundaries, many phases. Then you take a complete set and let the model select.
  • SOAP in one sentence: expand the neighbour density in radial functions and spherical harmonics, and take the power spectrum, which is rotation invariant.
  • If you are late: skip this slide. Say that ACSF and SOAP are the systematic version of today’s descriptors.

Checkpoint: is this a good descriptor?

Two minutes, in pairs. Which invariance does it break, and how do you repair it?

  1. The x coordinate of the column in pixels. → Not translation invariant. Use distances to neighbours.
  2. The angle of the nearest neighbour against the scan direction. → Not rotation invariant. Use the angle between two neighbours.
  3. The peak height in detector counts. → Not gain invariant. Divide by the local median, or calibrate the detector.
  4. The distance to the nearest neighbour in pixels. → Depends on the magnification. Convert to nm.
  5. The intensities of neighbours 1, 2, 3, 4 as four features. → Not permutation invariant. Sort them, or use mean and spread.
  6. The number of the image. → Invariant under everything in the image, and a pure leak. Remove it.
  • Procedure: give two minutes, then reveal one by one. Ask for the broken invariance first, then for the repair.
  • Cases 1 to 5 map to the five rows of the invariance table.
  • Case 5 is subtle. The order of the four nearest neighbours changes when two distances are nearly equal. Then the four features swap between two similar columns. A tree sees two different rows.
  • Case 6 is the bridge to Act 5. The image number is not physics. With a random split it is a perfect feature for everything that differs between images. bg and amp are image numbers in disguise.
  • Timing: 3 minutes. If you are late, do cases 1, 3 and 6.

Act 3 · Tree

How does a tree ask questions?

A decision tree is a sequence of yes/no questions

A tree of depth 3 on the six invariant descriptors, trained on all 1 934 labelled candidates. Each node shows the question, the number of rows and the class counts.

  • Purpose: read a real tree before we learn how to build one.
  • Read from the top. Each node asks one question about one descriptor: “is the value below a threshold?” Yes goes left, no goes right. A leaf predicts the class that has the most training rows in it.
  • The root question: “is d_spread at most 0.04?” That means: are the four neighbour distances nearly equal? 915 candidates say yes; most of them are substrate columns.
  • Follow a substrate column: regular neighbourhood, yes; I_rel above 0.83, so not a dim maximum; d4 at most 0.39 nm. It lands in a leaf with 795 rows, 98 % substrate.
  • Follow a noise maximum: d_spread above 0.04, and above 0.19; it lands in a leaf with 98 rows, 93 % “no column”.
  • The questions are physics that you can check. A perfect square lattice has a spread of zero. The threshold of 0.39 nm is just above the lattice parameter of LaAlO₃, 0.379 nm. The tree found these numbers from the data.
  • The weak leaves are the two film leaves on the right, with 69 % and 54 % purity. Three questions are not enough for the film.
  • With three levels the tree has at most 8 leaves. Its accuracy is 79 % on the training rows, and a tree of this depth gets 75 % on a new image.
  • What a tree is as a function: it cuts the descriptor space into boxes with edges parallel to the axes, and it predicts one constant in each box. A piecewise-constant function.
  • Three properties follow from this picture. (1) A tree needs no feature scaling: a threshold on d1 in nm or in pm gives the same split. (2) A tree finds thresholds and interactions: “if psi4 is high and d1 is large”. (3) A tree cannot extrapolate: outside the range of the training data the prediction stays constant.
  • The one point to land: a small tree is a model that you can read. That is rare, and it is the reason why trees are the first non-linear model to try.

Which question? The one that makes the children purer

1 Impurity of a node with class fractions \(p_k\): Gini \(I=1-\sum_k p_k^2\), entropy \(I=-\sum_k p_k\log_2 p_k\).

2 A split (feature \(j\), threshold \(t\)) sends \(N_L\) rows left and \(N_R\) rows right.

3 Impurity decrease: \(\Delta = I(\text{parent})-\frac{N_L}{N}I(L)-\frac{N_R}{N}I(R)\)

4 Choose the pair \((j,t)\) with the largest \(\Delta\). Regression: \(I\) = variance of \(y\) in the node.

Two classes: the impurity against the fraction \(p\) of one class.

  • Write the four lines on the board while the fragments appear. This is must-know statement 4.
  • Step 1: the impurity is zero when all rows of a node have one class. It is largest when the classes are mixed equally. Gini: the probability that two rows drawn at random from the node have different classes. Entropy: the mean number of bits needed to state the class.
  • Example at the root of our table: 942, 685 and 307 rows. The fractions are 0.49, 0.35, 0.16. Gini = 1 − 0.49² − 0.35² − 0.16² = 0.61.
  • Step 3: the impurity of the two children, weighted by their sizes, subtracted from the impurity of the parent. With entropy this is the information gain.
  • Step 4: for regression trees, the impurity is the variance of the target in the node. Then the split minimises the sum of squared errors, the loss of Week 4.
  • The figure: Gini and entropy have nearly the same shape. In practice they choose nearly the same splits. The misclassification error is a straight line. It is a bad criterion: a split that makes one child purer and the other less pure can leave the error unchanged, and Gini rewards it.
  • Misconception: “the tree maximises the accuracy.” It maximises the impurity decrease of one split at a time.

CART: greedy, recursive, cheap

The algorithm

  1. Start with all rows in one node.
  2. Find the split \((j,t)\) with the largest \(\Delta\).
  3. Split the rows. Repeat steps 2 and 3 in each child.
  4. Stop: node is pure, depth limit, or fewer than min_samples_leaf rows.
  5. Each leaf predicts its majority class (or its mean).
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier
tree = DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=3)
tree.fit(X_train, y_train)

What follows from it

  • Greedy: the best split now, not the best tree. The best tree is NP-hard to find.
  • No scaling needed: only the order of the values matters.
  • Thresholds and interactions are natural; smooth linear trends need many steps.
  • No extrapolation: constant outside the training range.
  • Unstable: a small change of the data can change the root, and then everything below it.
  • Purpose: the complete algorithm on one slide. CART = classification and regression trees (Breiman and co-workers, 1984).
  • Left column: read the five steps. This is a recursion. Each step uses only the rows of the current node.
  • Greedy: the algorithm never revises a split. A split that looks bad now and enables two very good splits below is not found. The XOR pattern is the standard example: no single split helps, two splits solve it.
  • No scaling: the split only compares values of one feature with a threshold. Any monotonic transform of a feature (log, scaling) gives the same tree. Compare with Week 4, where gradient descent needed standardised features.
  • No extrapolation, with a real example. With all 11 features, the random forest reaches 87 %, 77 % and 83 % on images B, C and D, and only 49 % on image A. Image A has the lowest counts of the four. Its amp values are below the range of the other three images, so every threshold on amp sends the columns of A to the wrong side.
  • Stopping rules are the regularisation of a tree: max_depth, min_samples_leaf, max_leaf_nodes. Cost-complexity pruning (ccp_alpha) grows a full tree and cuts it back.
  • Cost: about F · N log N for each level, for F features and N rows. A tree on our table trains in milliseconds.
  • The one point to land: a tree is a greedy, recursive search for impurity decrease. Its strengths and its weaknesses both come from that.

Act 4 · Ensemble

Why are many trees better than one?

Bagging: average many unstable trees

1 Bootstrap: draw \(N\) rows with replacement, \(B\) times. Train one deep tree on each sample.

2 Aggregate: average the \(B\) predictions (regression) or take the majority vote (classification).

3 Independent trees, each with variance \(\sigma^2\): \(\;\mathrm{Var}\big(\tfrac1B\sum_b f_b\big)=\sigma^2/B\). The bias does not change.

4 Correlated trees, pairwise correlation \(\rho\): \(\;\mathrm{Var}=\rho\,\sigma^2+\dfrac{1-\rho}{B}\,\sigma^2\;\xrightarrow{B\to\infty}\;\rho\,\sigma^2\)

More trees remove the second term only. The floor \(\rho\sigma^2\) stays: to go lower, make the trees less alike.

  • Write the four lines on the board. This is must-know statement 5. Bagging = bootstrap aggregating (Breiman 1996).
  • Steps 1 and 2: each tree sees another bootstrap sample, so each tree makes other errors at the class boundaries. The vote cancels a part of these errors.
  • Step 3: the variance of a mean of B independent values is σ²/B. This is the 1/√N law of Act 1 again: there we averaged frames, here we average trees.
  • Step 4: the trees are not independent, because all of them come from the same N rows. Derivation for the board: Var(Σf_b) = B σ² + B(B − 1) ρσ². Divide by B². The result is ρσ² + (1 − ρ)σ²/B.
  • Read the formula. The second term goes to zero with more trees. The first term does not depend on B. With ρ = 0.6, even an infinite number of trees keeps 60 % of the variance of one tree.
  • Consequence 1: more trees never hurt a bagged ensemble; they only cost time. B is not a hyperparameter to tune; use enough.
  • Consequence 2: the next improvement must lower ρ. That is the random forest.
  • Averaging does not lower the bias. So bagging needs trees with a low bias: deep trees.

Random forest: decorrelate the trees

Random forest Breiman, Leo, (2001) = bagging + one more random choice:

  • at each split, test only a random subset of the features (max_features, default \(\sqrt F\) for classification)
  • a strong feature cannot be the root of every tree: \(\rho\) falls
  • each tree is a little worse; the average is better
  • out-of-bag score: each tree tests the 37 % of rows it did not see
from sklearn.ensemble import (
    RandomForestClassifier as RF)
rf = RF(300, oob_score=True)
rf.fit(X_train, y_train)
rf.oob_score_
  • Purpose: one idea, lower ρ, and its measured effect.
  • The idea: in bagging, every tree may use every feature at every split. If one feature is very strong, all trees start with it, and the trees look alike. The random forest lets each split see only a random subset of the features. For 6 features, √6 gives 2 features per split.
  • The numbers, left panel: the mean over the four held-out images, six invariant descriptors. One tree alone: 77.5 %. Bagging: 82.6 % with 30 trees, 82.8 % with 200; ρ = 0.60. Random forest: 83.9 % with 30 trees, 84.1 % with 200; ρ = 0.56.
  • Right panel: the formula of the last slide for these two values of ρ. The dotted line is ρ = 0, independent trees: that variance would go to zero.
  • Be honest about the size of the effect: one point. With only six features, of which three or four are informative, the room for decorrelation is small. With 50 or 500 descriptors the gain of the forest over bagging is larger.
  • Both curves saturate after about 30 to 100 trees. More trees do not overfit. Use 300 to 500 and stop thinking about it.
  • Out-of-bag score: each bootstrap sample leaves out about 37 % of the rows. Each row is predicted by the trees that did not see it. This is a free validation score. Warning for our data: the out-of-bag rows come from the same images as the training rows. The out-of-bag score is a random-split score. It has the same optimism as the random folds.
  • Hyperparameters that matter: max_features and min_samples_leaf. The forest is robust: the defaults are usually within one or two points of the best setting.
  • The one point to land: a random forest lowers the variance twice: by averaging, and by making the trees less alike.

Boosting: each small tree corrects the last error

1 Start with a constant: \(F_0(\mathbf x)=\bar y\). Loss \(\mathcal L=\sum_i \ell\big(y_i,F(\mathbf x_i)\big)\).

2 Pseudo-residuals: \(r_i=-\,\partial \ell/\partial F(\mathbf x_i)\). For the squared error: \(r_i=y_i-F(\mathbf x_i)\), the residual.

3 Fit a small tree \(h_m\) to the \(r_i\) (depth 1 to 6).

4 Update: \(F_m=F_{m-1}+\eta\,h_m\). Repeat steps 2 to 4.

Gradient descent of Week 4, \(\mathbf w\leftarrow\mathbf w-\eta\nabla\mathcal L\), but in function space: each tree is one gradient step.

  • Write the four lines. This is must-know statement 6 (Friedman 2001).
  • Bagging trains the trees in parallel and independently. Boosting trains them in sequence: each tree sees the errors of all trees before it.
  • Step 2: for the squared error, the negative gradient is the residual y − F. So “fit the next tree to the residuals” is a gradient step. For other losses the same recipe works with another gradient: log-loss for classification, Poisson deviance for counts, Huber for outliers. The noise model gives the loss, as in Week 4.
  • Step 3: the trees are small on purpose, often with 2 to 32 leaves. A small tree has a high bias and a low variance. Boosting lowers the bias step by step.
  • Step 4: η is the learning rate, the same η as in Week 4. η = 1 takes the full step. η = 0.1 takes a tenth, and needs about ten times more trees.
  • The analogy in one sentence: gradient descent changes the weight vector against the gradient. Boosting changes the function against the gradient, and a tree is the approximation of that gradient that we can store.
  • Difference from bagging that matters: a boosted ensemble can overfit when it has too many trees. So the number of trees is a hyperparameter here.
  • Misconception: “boosting is a better random forest.” It attacks the bias, the forest attacks the variance. Which one wins depends on the data.

The rules

  1. Start with the majority class and a linear model.
  2. Use the same grouped folds for every model.
  3. A complex model must beat the baseline by more than the spread between the folds.
  4. If it does not: improve the descriptors, not the model.

With all 11 features, logistic regression beats the random forest by 6 points. Image A alone: forest 49 %, logistic 63 %.

  • Purpose: must-know statement 7. “Trees first” is a default; the ladder decides.
  • Column 1, all 11 features: the raw features are in the table together with the invariant ones. Logistic regression: 80 %. Random forest: 74 %. Boosting: 67 %. The more flexible the model, the worse it is. Why?
  • A flexible model uses every feature that lowers the training loss. The raw intensities lower it very well, so the trees split on them. On a new image these splits are wrong. The linear model has one weight for each feature and cannot build “if amp is in this narrow range” rules, so it suffers less.
  • Image A makes it visible. It has the lowest counts. All its amp values are below the range of the other images. The forest cannot extrapolate: 49 % on image A. The logistic model: 63 %.
  • Column 2, six invariant descriptors: now the forest wins, 84 % against 75 %. The classes are not linearly separable in these six numbers; the tree thresholds help.
  • Column 3, the symmetry functions: the linear model reaches 82 %, the forest 84 %. Better descriptors make the simple model nearly as good as the complex one. This is the general pattern.
  • Rule 3: the spread between the four folds is large here. The forest with invariant descriptors has 75 %, 87 %, 90 % and 86 % on images A to D. A difference of two points between two models is inside this spread. With four groups you cannot claim it.
  • Next week the ladder gets one more step: a neural network. It must beat these numbers on the same folds.
  • The one point to land: every model is a rung of one ladder, measured on the same grouped folds. The baseline is the reference for every claim.

Checkpoint: validation

Which descriptors does the model use?

Two importances: impurity and permutation

Random forest on all 11 features plus one column of pure random numbers (“noise”). Left: impurity importance. Middle: permutation importance on the training images. Right: permutation importance on a new image (bars: mean of four images; lines: spread).

  • Purpose: two definitions of “important”, and why the data that you compute them on matters.
  • Impurity importance (MDI, mean decrease in impurity): add the impurity decrease Δ of every split that uses the feature, over all trees. It is free: rf.feature_importances_. It is computed from the training data only.
  • Permutation importance: take the trained model. Shuffle one column of the test table, so that it has no relation to the labels. Measure how far the score falls. Repeat for each feature. sklearn.inspection.permutation_importance.
  • Left panel, MDI. The yellow bar is a column of random numbers. It gets 1.2 % of the impurity decrease. Deep trees split on anything in small nodes, also on noise. The bias is stronger for continuous features with many different values.
  • The red bar is bg, the background under the peak. MDI: 8 %, a middle rank. Middle panel, permutation on the training images: bg is the most important feature, 3.6 points. Right panel, permutation on a new image: −0.1 points. Zero.
  • So the model uses bg strongly, and bg does not help on a new image. Both statements are true. Importance on the training images tells you what the model uses. Importance on held-out groups tells you what generalises.
  • The same for amp: third in the impurity ranking, nearly zero on a new image. On the right, the top places go to d1, d_spread, contrast and I_rel.
  • Look at the lines on the right panel: the spread between the four images is as large as many bars. Do not rank features that differ by less than this spread.
  • Misconception: “feature importance tells me what matters physically.” It tells you what this model uses on this data.
  • The one point to land: compute permutation importance on held-out groups, and include a noise column as the zero of the scale.

Correlated descriptors hide each other

Left: permutation importance on a new image for the six invariant descriptors, and again after adding one or two exact copies of d4 to the table. Right: accuracy when the feature is removed and the model is trained again.

d4 falls from 11 points to 2.5 to 0 when copies exist. Remove d1 or d4: −1 point. Remove both: −6 points.

  • Purpose: the main trap of permutation importance. This is must-know statement 9.
  • The experiment: d4 is the most important descriptor, 11 points. Add an exact copy of the d4 column to the table and train again. The importance of d4 falls to 2.5 points. With two copies it is zero. The information is the same in all three tables.
  • Why? The forest spreads its splits over the copies. When you shuffle one copy, the other copies still carry the information, and the score does not fall.
  • Real data have no exact copies, but they have correlations. d1 and d4 correlate with r = 0.78. Right panel: remove d1 and train again: 84.1 → 82.9 %. Remove d4: 83.2 %. Each one looks nearly useless. Remove both: 77.8 %. Together they carry 6 points.
  • A second problem of the shuffle: it makes rows that cannot exist. A column with a nearest-neighbour distance of 0.38 nm and a mean distance of 0.28 nm is not possible; the mean of four distances cannot be below the smallest one. The model has never seen such rows, and its answer there has no meaning. The name is “off-manifold” data.
  • The cure: put correlated descriptors into one group, and permute or remove the group together. Find the groups with a correlation matrix or with hierarchical clustering of the features.
  • “Drop and retrain” is the honest and expensive version: one new model for each feature group.
  • Misconception: “low importance means that I can delete the feature.” It can mean that another feature holds the same information.
  • The one point to land: importance belongs to groups of correlated features, not to single features.

SHAP: divide one prediction among the descriptors

1 One prediction, one sum: \(f(\mathbf x)=\phi_0+\sum_j\phi_j\)

2 \(\phi_0\) = the mean prediction over the background data.

3 \(\phi_j\) = the mean change of the prediction when feature \(j\) joins, averaged over all orders of the features (Shapley value).

4 TreeSHAP: exact for trees in polynomial time. No sampling.

Each point: one candidate of image B. Colour: the value of the descriptor.

  • Purpose: from “which features matter in general” to “why this prediction”. Must-know statement 10.
  • Step 1: SHAP writes one prediction as a base value plus one signed number for each feature. The sum is exact. The name of this property is completeness, or local accuracy.
  • Step 3, the idea from game theory: the features are players, the prediction is the payout. The contribution of a feature depends on which features are already known. So take the mean over all orders. Lundberg and Lee, 2017.
  • Step 4: for a general model this needs a sum over 2^F subsets. For trees, an exact algorithm exists that follows the paths of the trees: TreeSHAP, Lundberg and co-workers, 2020. For our forest and 580 columns it takes seconds.
  • The figure, the summary plot: one row per descriptor, one point per column of image B. Horizontal position: φ for the class “film column”. Right of zero: this descriptor pushes the probability of “film” up for this column. Colour: the value of the descriptor, red is high.
  • Read d_spread: blue points, a regular neighbourhood, are on the left: −0.13, against “film”. This is the substrate. Red points are on the right. Read d1: red points, a large nearest distance, are left of zero: the substrate has 0.37 nm. Blue points split into two groups: a moderately small distance supports “film”; a very small distance is on the far left, against “film”: these are noise maxima.
  • So a SHAP plot also shows non-monotonic relations. d4 is the clearest: high values appear on both sides.
  • Global importance from SHAP: the mean of |φ| for each descriptor. It is a third importance measure, and it has the same problem with correlated features: the credit is divided among them.
  • The one point to land: SHAP is an exact accounting of one prediction of the model.

Click a column: why this prediction?

  • Purpose: read single explanations on the image, and check the sum rule with numbers.
  • The image: image B, which the forest did not see in training. The colour of each point is the predicted class. A white ring marks a wrong prediction.
  • Demo, step 1: the selected film column near the interface. Read the waterfall from the top. φ₀ = 0.345: the mean predicted probability of “film”. d_spread = 0.144 adds 0.115. d1 = 0.289 nm adds 0.107. d4 = 0.342 nm takes away 0.097. The bars end at 0.655, the prediction. Check line: 0.345 + 0.311 = 0.655.
  • Demo, step 2: click a column deep in the substrate. Switch the class to “substrate”. All bars point up: a regular square neighbourhood with the right distances.
  • Demo, step 3: click a column with a white ring, a wrong prediction, at the border of the amorphous layer. Look for the descriptor that pushed it to the wrong class. Often psi4 and d_spread say “disordered”, and the label says “film”. The explanation shows that the model and the label disagree about what a film column is.
  • Demo, step 4: click one substrate column at the edge of the image. Its neighbours are cut off by the image border, so its descriptors are wrong. The explanation finds a data problem, not a physics result. This is the most common use of SHAP in practice: debugging.
  • Ask: “Does a large φ for d1 mean that the distance causes the class?” No. Next slide.
  • The one point to land: an explanation is a tool to find errors in the data, the labels and the descriptors.

Explanation is not causation

What SHAP and importance tell you

  • which inputs this model uses
  • how one prediction divides among the inputs
  • where the model uses a nuisance (bg, amp): a leak detector
  • where the labels and the model disagree

What they do not tell you

  • what causes the property in the specimen
  • what happens if you change a descriptor in the experiment
  • anything stable under correlated features: the credit is divided
  • anything independent of the background data that defines \(\phi_0\)

A model can be right for the wrong reason. Only an experiment or a physical model tests a cause.

  • Purpose: set the limit of everything in this act. The second half of must-know statement 10.
  • Left column: these are statements about the model. They are useful. The forest with raw features had a high score on random folds, and its explanation said “background”. That finding alone would have told us about the leak, without a second split.
  • Right column, point 1 and 2: a descriptor can be a proxy. In our data the thickness of the specimen changes the peak height and also the visibility of the film columns. A model that uses the peak height is not a model of the film.
  • Point 3: with correlated descriptors, another forest with another random seed can divide the credit differently between d1 and d4. Report the group.
  • Point 4: φ₀ is the mean prediction over a background data set. Change the background, for example from “all images” to “substrate columns only”, and every φ changes. Always state the background. For our figure: the training images of the forest, with the path-dependent TreeSHAP.
  • Good practice, as a list for the report: (1) the model beats the baseline on grouped folds; (2) permutation importance on held-out groups, with a noise column; (3) correlated features in groups; (4) SHAP for single cases, with the background stated; (5) a check against physics: does the sign make sense?
  • The one point to land: explain the model to find its errors. Do not sell the explanation as physics.
  • If you are late: skip this slide and say its claim in one sentence.

Must-know for the exam

1 Classical ML needs a feature table: rows = objects, columns = descriptors. Detection errors propagate into it.

2 Column finding: smooth → local maxima → 2-D Gaussian fit. Precision ∝ 1/√dose; Poisson likelihood at low dose.

3 Good descriptors are invariant (translation, rotation, permutation, gain): ratios like \(I_\text{rel}\), distances, ACSF/SOAP.

4 Tree: greedy split with the largest \(\Delta = I(\text{parent})-\frac{N_L}{N}I(L)-\frac{N_R}{N}I(R)\). No scaling, no extrapolation, high variance.

5 Average of \(B\) trees: variance \(\rho\sigma^2+\frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2\). A random forest lowers \(\rho\) with random feature subsets.

6 Boosting = gradient descent in function space: fit a small tree to \(-\partial\mathcal L/\partial F\), add \(\eta h\). Early stopping.

7 Baseline ladder on identical grouped folds: majority → linear → forest/boosting. Good descriptors help the linear model.

8 Cross-validate by image, session or specimen. Features that identify the image inflate random-split scores.

9 MDI is biased (noise gets credit). Permutation on held-out groups; permute correlated features together.

10 SHAP: \(f(\mathbf x)=\phi_0+\sum_j\phi_j\), exact for trees. It explains the model, not the cause; it depends on the background.

Trees are not rotation invariant

A tree splits along the axes of the table. A boundary that is diagonal in the feature space needs many steps.

  • A tree splits on one feature at a time. A class boundary along a diagonal, for example “d1 − d4 > 0”, needs a staircase of many splits.
  • For tables this is often an advantage: the columns have a meaning, and physical thresholds apply to single quantities. Grinsztajn and co-workers name this as one reason why trees beat neural networks on tables.
  • If you know that a combination matters, for example a ratio or a difference, add it as a descriptor. One good descriptor replaces many splits.

Out-of-bag error and regression trees

Out-of-bag (OOB)

  • a bootstrap sample omits a row with probability \((1-1/N)^N\to e^{-1}\approx 37\,\%\)
  • predict each row with the trees that did not see it
  • a validation score without a second data set
  • grouped data: OOB rows share the image with training rows → optimistic, like a random split

Regression trees

  • impurity = variance of \(y\) in the node
  • a leaf predicts the mean of its rows
  • the split minimises the sum of squared errors
  • other losses: absolute error (median leaf), Poisson deviance (counts)
  • the prediction is piecewise constant: no extrapolation
  • OOB is useful for a fast check during the development. For the final claim, use the grouped split.
  • Regression trees are the weak learners of gradient boosting, also for classification: the trees fit the pseudo-residuals, which are real numbers.
